Stop Selling the ERP: How to Actually Get Employee Buy-In

PUBLISHED July 29, 2026 · GUIDE / Strategy

The typical ERP rollout begins with a PowerPoint deck, a CEO declaring a 'new era,' and a mandatory training session where nobody asks real questions. Six months later, users are running shadow systems because the new software doesn't match how they actually work. That's a failure of change management, not technology.

The problem isn't that employees resist change — it's that they resist untested promises. When a vendor says 'the system will save you hours,' but the warehouse team still gets daily errors, trust evaporates. The fix is counterintuitive: stop selling the system. Instead, admit the pain points upfront. Let the finance team test the new GL reconciliation module before go-live. Let operations leaders kill features that don't fit their workflow. That kind of authority builds buy-in faster than any executive memo.

Early adopters need to be skeptics, not cheerleaders. Identify the most vocal critics — the ones who 'never liked the old system either' and will point out every flaw. Give them a sandbox environment with real data. Let them break things. Their feedback, when actually acted upon, becomes the strongest endorsement you can get. Meanwhile, resist the urge to customize every complaint away. Some process pain is actually good if it forces discipline.

Training should happen in context, not in a generic lab. Show users their actual screens, their actual data, their actual exceptions. If the new system can't handle the weird customer credit scenario they deal with weekly, they need to know that — and know the workaround — before day one. Avoid the temptation to promise a 'smooth transition.' Instead, promise that problems will be addressed quickly. That's what actually earns trust. Buy-in isn't a checkbox. It's what happens when you let people see the system's warts and still choose to use it because the alternative is worse.
