How to Choose an ERP Implementation Partner: Buyer's Checklist

PUBLISHED September 30, 2026 · GUIDE / Selection

The software is rarely what kills an ERP project. The implementation partner usually is — through understaffed teams, consultants learning your business on your dime, and change orders that were baked into the pricing model from the start. Choosing that partner is the most consequential decision you make after picking the platform, and most buyers spend a fraction of the time on it that they spent sitting through demos.

Start by knowing which species of partner you're talking to. There are the vendor's own consulting arms, the global systems integrators, regional firms with deep industry roots, offshore-heavy delivery shops, and the two-person boutique run by a former SAP or NetSuite architect. Their failure modes differ. Globals can rotate your best people onto a bigger account without warning. Boutiques can be brilliant until the workload doubles and there is no bench behind the founder.

Watch for the reseller conflict. Plenty of partners earn margin on the licenses they sell you, which means every extra module, every added user tier, and every adjacent cloud product is revenue for them. That doesn't make them dishonest. It does mean you should keep your own requirements list and budget envelope in front of you during scoping, not just their recommendations.

A famous logo on the proposal tells you almost nothing. What matters is whether the people who wrote the proposal are the people who show up in week three. Ask, in writing, for the names and CVs of the actual project manager, the lead consultant for your functional area, and the technical architect.

Then put teeth behind it in the contract: named key personnel, agreed day rates, and a clause requiring your written approval before anyone on that list is replaced. Without it, you get the A-team for the sales cycle and a rotating cast of juniors for the build.

Reference checks are where most buyers go soft. Calling three hand-picked customers, listening to how wonderful everything was, and moving on is theater. Ask for references from a project of similar size and industry, and also ask the partner to name an engagement where the relationship went sideways. The good ones will have one, and their account of it is more informative than the glowing ones.

On those calls, ask three questions: who actually did the work, how many change orders hit you, and would you hire them again for the same scope? Then ask for the project manager from that engagement by name. If that person has since left the firm, you've learned something about retention that no capability deck will tell you.

Consider running a paid blueprint or discovery phase with two finalists before committing to the full program. It costs money and a few weeks. In exchange, you watch how each one scopes, how they handle an ambiguous requirement, and who they assign — before you're locked into a multi-year relationship.

The fixed-price versus time-and-materials debate is mostly a distraction. Fixed price just relocates risk, and partners price it back in through narrow scope assumptions. The document that decides whether you stay on budget is the assumptions and exclusions list. Read it line by line and challenge anything that sounds like a boundary they'll want to charge you to cross later.

Be suspicious of a partner who says yes to every customization you float. Heavy modification of core tables and logic creates upgrade debt you'll pay for years, and a partner compensated by the hour has no incentive to talk you out of it. The integrator who pushes back on your wishlist, and can explain the standard-process alternative, is usually the safer hire.

Ask who does the data migration, and insist on a real answer. Converting chart of accounts, item masters, open orders, and historical transactions is tedious work that partners like to leave vague because it's hard to estimate and hard to staff. If it's time-and-materials and unpriced, that's where the surprise invoice lives.

Nail down go-live and what follows. What does hypercare look like, how long does it last, who pays for it, and what happens when the first month-end close goes badly? Ask to see a sample cutover plan and a test strategy from a comparable project. If they can't produce either, they're improvising on your schedule.

Insist on a plan for turning the system over to your team: documentation, knowledge transfer sessions, and a named internal successor for each consultant. A partner whose business model depends on you calling them forever will not volunteer this. Put a date on it anyway.

None of this rescues a client that won't make decisions. You need your own project manager with authority to say no, business process owners who attend design workshops and settle disputes, and an executive sponsor who shows up when the steering committee has to break a tie. Partners routinely get blamed for timelines that slipped because the customer couldn't sign off on a process design for six weeks.

Two more practical checks. Industry depth: ask how many clients of your size and sector they've implemented for in the last three years, and ask to speak to one. Generalist firms win deals on breadth, then learn your warehouse layout on your budget. Delivery model: get the onshore/offshore split, the time zones involved, and who owns quality on the offshore side. A heavily offshore team is cheaper and slower to react. You're buying a tradeoff, so know which one you bought.

Write the exit before you write the celebration. Termination for convenience, transition assistance, ownership of deliverables and configuration documentation, and a clear warranty period after go-live. A partner confident in its delivery won't flinch at those terms. The one that argues against every accountability clause is telling you what it expects to happen.

Most shortlists get decided on the hourly rate and the polish of the deck. The projects that go well get decided on the named team, the assumptions list, and the reference call nobody else bothered to make.
