ERP for Distribution: Warehouse and Logistics Integration

PUBLISHED October 05, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Warehouse Integration

Most distribution companies start ERP selection with finance and order management, then assume warehouse and logistics will follow. That assumption causes trouble. ERP systems like NetSuite, Acumatica, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central, Sage X3, Epicor, Infor, and SAP Business One all track inventory value, purchasing, sales orders, and landed cost. Some include basic bin locations or RF scanning through add-on modules. Fewer systems handle wave picking, cartonization, yard management, or complex carrier rating without a dedicated WMS or TMS. The boundary between ERP and execution systems is the first thing to map.

Integration starts with master data. Item numbers, descriptions, units of measure, barcodes, lot and serial rules, expiry dates, warehouse locations, and bin capacity all need a single owner. In practice, ERP usually owns the financial item and purchasing unit, while WMS owns bin-level quantity and handling unit. If the two disagree on UOM conversion, a case of 12 can become 144 units in the ledger or a picker scans the wrong label. Inventory reconciliation should run at least daily, and cycle counts need a workflow that pushes adjustments back to ERP with reason codes, not a manual journal entry at month end.

Order flow is where integration earns its keep. ERP releases orders to WMS; WMS confirms picks, packs, and shipments; ERP invoices. In EDI-heavy distribution, that means 940 warehouse shipping orders, 945 shipping advice, 856 advance ship notices, 810 invoices, and 997 acknowledgements. Carrier integration adds rate shopping, label printing, manifesting, and tracking updates. Many ERP suites have parcel integrations for small-volume shippers, but high-volume or multi-carrier operations often need a TMS or parcel engine. The same pattern applies to 3PLs and drop ship: inventory feeds, receipt confirmations, and shipment notices must flow without someone retyping a tracking number.

Architecture choices matter more than vendor demos. Prebuilt connectors work when your ERP and WMS are common pairs, such as NetSuite with a partner WMS or Dynamics 365 with a warehouse app. Middleware like Boomi, Celigo, Jitterbit, MuleSoft, or Workato gives flexibility but adds another system to monitor. APIs and webhooks handle real-time events, while flat files and database triggers still run many warehouses. Real-time integration feels safer for inventory accuracy, but it also couples systems: an ERP outage can stop picking. Batch integration is simpler, but a nightly inventory sync can cause overselling on the web store. Pick the cadence per data domain, not one setting for everything.

Ask vendors hard questions. Does the ERP have a native WMS, or is it a partner add-on? Can it handle catch weight, lot and serial traceability, expiry, hazmat, and cross-docking? Does it support RF scanners, voice picking, or license plate inventory? What TMS and carrier connections exist? How are integration errors surfaced, retried, and audited? Who owns item and location master data? Get references from distributors with similar SKU counts, order profiles, and warehouse layouts. Pricing varies significantly by vendor and deployment size. Implementation effort tracks integration scope, data quality, and dock complexity, not just the number of users.

My bias: if distribution is the core of your business, choose the WMS and TMS that fit the warehouse first, then make ERP integrate with them. ERP should be the system of record for financials, procurement, order management, and inventory valuation. The warehouse system should own execution: receiving, putaway, picking, packing, shipping, and returns. When that boundary is clear, integration is a set of well-defined messages. When it is blurry, every shortage becomes an argument between two systems and three departments.
