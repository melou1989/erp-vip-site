IFS vs Infor for Asset-Intensive Industries: ERP Buyer's Guide

PUBLISHED September 25, 2026 · COMPARISON / Vendor Comparison

IFS and Infor both sell ERP to companies whose balance sheets depend on expensive equipment, maintenance schedules, and field crews. The overlap is real. If you run a utility, an airline, a mining operation, or a service contractor with thousands of field assets, both vendors will show up on your shortlist. But they arrive with different histories, different architectures, and different ideas about what asset-intensive actually means.

IFS Cloud is the current story: one product line that combines ERP, enterprise asset management, field service management, and project accounting. That matters for asset-intensive firms because work orders, maintenance plans, and project costs often move through the same operational flow. IFS has deep roots in aerospace and defense, energy, utilities, construction, and service-heavy manufacturing. Its field service scheduling is a genuine differentiator for companies dispatching technicians to remote or customer sites. The trade-off is regional: IFS is less of a household name in some markets, and its partner bench for niche integrations is thinner than the largest ERP vendors.

Infor comes at the same market from a portfolio. Infor EAM, with roots in Datastream and MP2, is the asset management workhorse. Infor CloudSuite EAM adds a modern interface, mobility, GIS and linear asset support, and hooks into Infor OS. For manufacturers, Infor CloudSuite Industrial and other industry suites cover the production side. Infor's strategy uses industry clouds rather than one monolithic product, so buyers need to map which CloudSuite actually fits their operation. Asset management depth is credible, especially for linear assets, utilities, transportation, and public infrastructure. The wider Infor stack can still feel like a collection of acquired products under a common sign-in, and integration quality depends on how much of the Infor OS layer you adopt.

The practical fork is project-centric work versus asset-centric maintenance. IFS tends to win when the asset is tied to a project, a contract, or a field service agreement. Think EPC firms, aviation maintenance, telecom infrastructure, or complex equipment servicers. Its project management and contract accounting are part of the core, not bolted on. Infor EAM can handle those patterns too, but if project profitability and revenue recognition are central, you may end up combining Infor EAM with another ERP or finance system. That adds integration and reconciliation work.

Both vendors are cloud-first now, but the architectures differ. IFS Cloud is delivered as a service with a vendor-managed update cadence; customization is possible but constrained. Infor offers cloud deployment through Infor CloudSuite on AWS, with Infor OS providing integration, workflow, document management, and Coleman AI. Infor's API and integration story is decent, but older on-prem EAM versions remain common. Buyers should ask about upgrade paths from legacy Infor EAM or IFS Applications, not just the demo environment.

Implementation risk is where the comparison gets real. IFS relies on a smaller set of specialist partners; that can mean better focus but less negotiating room on rates. Infor has a wider partner network, but quality varies by region and by product line. Neither vendor publishes a simple price list. Licensing is typically based on named users, concurrent users, modules, and asset counts or equipment records; pricing varies significantly by vendor, deployment size, and contract structure. A mid-sized utility and a global airline will not see the same quote.

Pick IFS if you want one system for projects, assets, field service, and finance, and you value a tighter product roadmap. Pick Infor if your core need is EAM with strong linear asset and GIS capabilities, if you already run other Infor products, or if you want a broad industry cloud portfolio. Do not pick either based on a generic feature checklist. Build scenarios: a breakdown repair, a planned outage, a warranty claim, a project milestone billing, a subcontractor invoice. Then make the vendors show those end to end.

The uncomfortable truth is that both systems can run an asset-intensive business. The failure mode is usually not the software's feature list. It is bad master data, unclear ownership of maintenance and finance processes, and an implementation partner that treats EAM as a back-office module. Ask for reference calls with companies that have similar asset counts, regulatory regimes, and union or safety constraints. Then ask what they would do differently.
