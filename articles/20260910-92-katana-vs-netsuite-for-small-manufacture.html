Katana vs NetSuite for Small Manufacturers: Which Fits?

PUBLISHED September 10, 2026 · COMPARISON / Comparison

Katana and NetSuite get compared constantly, mostly because both show up when a small manufacturer searches for ERP software. They are not the same category of product, and treating them as head-to-head options is where most of the confusion starts. Katana is a production and inventory layer. NetSuite is an accounting system with manufacturing built around it. That difference decides almost everything else.

Katana was built around a specific workflow: a shop that makes physical goods, tracks components, and keeps its books in QuickBooks Online or Xero. You create sales orders, build BOMs, issue work orders, consume materials, log production output, and watch stock move across warehouses. It pushes invoices and inventory adjustments into the accounting system. Shop floor operators get a stripped-down view; whoever runs purchasing sees reorder points and shortages.

The appeal is speed. A small manufacturer can often get Katana running without hiring a consultant. Import your item list, connect Shopify or WooCommerce, link the accounting file, start building. Pricing scales by plan tier and user count, and while it varies quite a bit by headcount and feature level, the commitment is a subscription rather than a multi-year services engagement. For a 15-person shop, that matters more than any feature comparison chart.

Where Katana runs out of road is the moment the business needs a general ledger it trusts. There is no chart of accounts, no consolidation, no multi-entity structure. Costing is thin. If your controller wants standard cost variances, landed cost allocation by shipment, or WIP accounting that survives an audit, you will be running spreadsheets beside the tool. Batch and lot tracking exists, but high-volume traceability and regulatory reporting are not what it was designed for.

NetSuite is the opposite shape. The GL is the center of gravity: multi-currency, multi-entity consolidation, revenue recognition, tax, AP and AR, budgeting, and a reporting layer finance teams actually use for board packs. Because the books and the inventory sit in one database, inventory valuation, COGS, and margin reporting reconcile by construction. For a CFO who is tired of tying out QuickBooks against a spreadsheet of open work orders, that is the entire pitch.

Manufacturing in NetSuite arrives through Advanced Manufacturing and the Manufacturing Edition packaging: work orders, BOMs with routings, MRP, demand planning, WIP, shop floor transactions. It is capable, but it is not a dedicated MES. Scheduling, quality management, and machine-level data collection frequently come from partners or add-ons. And the implementation is not self-serve. You hire a partner, you spend months on configuration and data migration, and you pay for all of it.

NetSuite pricing is quoted, not published. It varies significantly by modules, user count, entities, and negotiation timing, notoriously so at the end of Oracle's fiscal quarters. Contracts are typically annual with auto-renewal and an uplift clause. Assume the implementation and the first year of subscription land in the same ballpark, and that post-go-live support becomes a permanent line item rather than a one-time cost.

So who picks Katana? A 10-to-50 person manufacturer doing light assembly or make-to-order work, selling through ecommerce plus a few wholesale accounts, with a bookkeeper on QuickBooks and no auditor asking hard questions. If your biggest pain is that nobody knows what is on the shelf and ship dates keep slipping, Katana fixes that in weeks. If your biggest pain is that the financials are wrong, Katana does not touch it.

Who picks NetSuite? Companies with genuine financial complexity: multiple legal entities, intercompany transactions, foreign currency, inventory that needs defensible valuation, EDI requirements from retail customers, or a raise or audit on the horizon. Also companies that have already outgrown a bolt-on. When you have three systems and a month-end reconciliation ritual, one integrated platform starts looking cheap.

The awkward middle is where most evaluations actually land. A 60-person manufacturer with two entities, a Shopify store, and a mix of make-to-stock and custom jobs often finds Katana too limited and NetSuite too heavy, too expensive, and too slow to implement. That is the zone where Acumatica, Odoo, Epicor Kinetic, Global Shop Solutions, and a cluster of smaller job-shop systems compete. Worth a look before signing a multi-year NetSuite agreement you cannot unwind.

Switching costs deserve more weight than feature grids get. Moving off Katana is annoying but survivable, a few weeks of exports and re-entry. Moving off NetSuite is a project with its own budget. That asymmetry means the safer sequence for many small manufacturers is Katana now and reassess at the next inflection point, rather than buying NetSuite for a scale you have not reached. The counterargument is real: migrating twice costs more than migrating once, and the second migration always lands in your busiest quarter.

One practical test before you demo anything. Ask your controller to describe month-end close, then ask your production manager to describe how a rush order gets scheduled. If the controller's answer includes the word reconcile more than twice, you are closer to NetSuite than you think. If the production manager is the one losing sleep, Katana is probably the higher-return purchase.
