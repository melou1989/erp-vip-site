ERP Change Management: How to Get Employee Buy-In

PUBLISHED October 01, 2026 · GUIDE / Strategy

Ask anyone who has cleaned up after a rough ERP go-live and you'll hear a version of the same sentence: the software did what it was supposed to do. The warehouse kept a spreadsheet on the side. Finance still ran the old close checklist in parallel. Six months and a lot of license spend later, the system of record was nominally the ERP and practically something else.

That gap is what change management exists to close, and it's the part of ERP delivery that gets cut first when the timeline tightens. Vendor methodologies acknowledge it — SAP Activate, Oracle's OUM, Microsoft's FastTrack all put organizational change management somewhere in the plan. Whether it survives contact with a compressed schedule is a different question.

Buy-in has a bad reputation because it's usually attempted as a communications exercise. A town hall, a newsletter, a login banner. None of that creates buy-in. It creates awareness, which is a cheaper and much easier thing to produce.

The Beckhard-Harris change equation is still the clearest way to think about this: Dissatisfaction × Vision × First Steps > Resistance. Note that it multiplies rather than adds. If any one term is close to zero, the product collapses no matter how strong the others are. Most ERP programs lean hard on Vision and skip First Steps entirely, then wonder why the energy from the kickoff doesn't survive Q2.

Dissatisfaction is the awkward one. The people most frustrated with the current process are often the same people who have been quietly compensating for its failures for years, and the new system tells them their workarounds were wrong. That's not a natural ally you've just recruited. It's someone whose expertise is being invalidated in public.

The group that actually decides whether your ERP lands is the first-line supervisor, not the executive sponsor. Employees don't ask the CFO whether the new system is real. They ask their team lead, and they ask in the break room. If that person says something like 'just keep doing it the old way until the smoke clears,' the project is finished, even if the steering committee doesn't find out for another four months.

Prosci's change research has ranked active, visible sponsorship from senior leaders at or near the top of success factors for years, and that finding holds up. But sponsorship and buy-in aren't the same thing. A CFO on a podium is sponsorship. A supervisor sitting next to an AP clerk at 5pm on close day, walking through how the new three-way match handles a partial receipt, is buy-in. Budget for the second one.

Super user networks do more heavy lifting than any training vendor. Pick the person on the floor everyone already asks when the label printer jams — not the most senior person, not the most enthusiastic volunteer. Give them early access to the build, a named contact at the integrator who answers within a day, and backfilled hours. Not a title. Not a gift card. Hours, taken off their actual job, with their manager told in writing that the ERP work comes first.

Resistance is information. The AP clerk who tells you the new system can't handle partial receipt matching is not being difficult; she may also be the only person who understands how many partial receipts your vendors actually generate. Test it in a sandbox with real data. If she's right, log the change request and tell her you logged it. If she's wrong, show her the screen. Either way she's been heard, and she'll report the outcome to her colleagues more credibly than any internal comms email.

Training fails in predictable ways. A generic 'Introduction to ERP Navigation' session for a mixed room of warehouse pickers and controllers wastes both groups' morning. Training delivered three months before go-live leaks away before anyone touches a transaction. Training on a demo instance that doesn't match the configured system, with sample data that doesn't match their data, produces a room full of people who can navigate and can't work. Keep it role-based, close to cutover, and running against the real configuration.

The same five people are usually asked to write test scripts, clean vendor master data, sit through design workshops, and deliver training — while still closing the month. Something gives, and it's almost always the ERP work, because month-end has a hard deadline and the project has a plan that everyone assumes will absorb the slippage. Backfill these roles or accept that data clean-up will happen in the last three weeks, badly.

Productivity drops after cutover. This is normal, it's well documented, and it's survivable. Say so out loud, in advance, in writing, with an expected recovery window and a named hypercare contact. A dip that was predicted reads as a project phase. A dip that wasn't reads as a crisis, and crises produce the shadow spreadsheets and parallel processes you'll spend the next two years unwinding.

Logins are not adoption. Measure the things that actually hurt: what share of invoices go directly into the ERP versus being scanned and keyed by a shared-services team; how many manual journal entries land after cutover; how many offline spreadsheets still feed the management pack. Those numbers tell you where the workarounds live, and they tell you which supervisor's team needs another visit from the super user.

Sponsorship can't be delegated to a steering committee made up of the sponsor's peers. ERP programs require dozens of trade-off decisions, and someone with budget authority has to make them and be seen making them. Projects that route every disagreement to a committee of equals stall. Stalling is what kills ERP implementations — not the software, not the integrator, not the price.

There is no moment where buy-in is achieved and the work stops. It's rebuilt weekly, and a single two-day outage where nobody tells the user community why can undo a quarter's worth of it. Plan the recovery communication for that outage before it happens, because it will.
