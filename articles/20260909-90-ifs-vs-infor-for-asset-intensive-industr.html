IFS vs Infor for Asset-Intensive Industries: Which Fits?

PUBLISHED September 09, 2026 · COMPARISON / Vendor Comparison

For asset-intensive industries—aviation, energy, water utilities, complex manufacturing—the ERP decision has always been about whether the software understands that a maintenance work order matters as much as a sales order. IFS and Infor represent two very different answers to that question. One embeds asset management in its core suite from day one; the other offers a portfolio of mature applications you wire together.

IFS built its reputation in aerospace, defense, and energy, where projects and service contracts drive revenue. Its early architecture was built around assets and configurations, not just inventory items. That heritage still shows: IFS Cloud gives you the asset hierarchy, maintenance history, supply chain, and financials all living on one data backbone, which is a real advantage when a field service report has to affect a capital ledger.

Infor is a different animal, especially after becoming part of Koch Industries. Their portfolio is assembled from several acquired pedigrees: CloudSuite Industrial, CloudSuite M3, Infor LN, and the separate Infor EAM product that started life as Datastream Systems. Datastream was an excellent CMMS for work orders and parts management, but it was never an ERP. It was designed to sit beside one. That creates a fundamental alignment question for buyers.

That distinction matters in daily operations. With IFS, finance, maintenance, and supply chain staff are looking at the same transactional record. When you perform a warranty repair or an engine overhaul, the cost structure appears immediately in project accounting or asset accounts. Infor can deliver similar outcomes, but it often requires deliberate integration between its ERP and EAM offerings—and in many existing sites, the ERP is still an older on-premise LN or SyteLine install that needs a middleware bridge to the cloud.

In practice, IFS tends to win the comparison when your asset-heavy operation is also project-heavy: MRO providers, defense contractors, energy companies doing turnarounds, and any business that needs to track capital costs against an asset hierarchy across multiple sites. IFS's scheduling engine and work order handling have real teeth when you need a single report that connects revenue, cost, and asset health.

Infor's strongest hand is when you already fit one of its industry-specific ERPs well—a process manufacturer on CloudSuite M3, or a discreet engineer-to-order business on LN—and you are primarily replacing a legacy maintenance system. Infor EAM has decades of maintenance logic behind it and pairs naturally with those back ends. But it's not a shortcut to integrate with your broader financial workflows. You'll be building that bridge yourself.

There is also the cloud maturity question. IFS is a single, modern product line running on one cloud architecture, which feels simpler for a company starting fresh. Infor has huge scale from the inherited Baan, SyteLine, and M3 install bases, but it is still moving many workloads to its modern multi-tenant cloud at different speeds. That variation matters when you are pacing an upgrade over three to five years.

If you're asset-heavy with a project or service backdrop, IFS gives you a shorter path between a breakdown and its financial consequence. If you already run an Infor supply chain and just need a better maintenance workplace, Infor EAM deserves a look. But don't confuse the two approaches: one is an asset-centric ERP, the other is an ERP plus an asset management product.
