Katana vs NetSuite for Small Manufacturers: ERP Comparison

PUBLISHED September 26, 2026 · COMPARISON / Vendor Comparison

Katana and NetSuite show up on the same shortlist because both call themselves manufacturing ERP, but they are not the same kind of product. Katana is a focused cloud system for small operations that need live inventory, production planning, and order flow without a finance-led implementation project. NetSuite is a broad business suite: financials, inventory, manufacturing, CRM, ecommerce, and multi-entity consolidation in one platform. For a small manufacturer, the choice usually comes down to how much of that breadth you will actually use.

Katana shines in make-to-order, assemble-to-order, and light batch production. If your BOMs are simple or moderate, your shop floor tracks work orders on screens instead of paper travelers, and your biggest pain is knowing what you can build after current stock and incoming purchase orders, Katana covers that ground. It connects well with ecommerce tools like Shopify and accounting packages like QuickBooks or Xero, which matters if you sell direct and do not want to abandon the books you already trust.

The trade-off is depth. Katana is not built for complex routings, advanced cost accounting, global consolidation, or highly regulated traceability. If you need lot and serial tracking at granular levels, demand planning across multiple warehouses, or revenue recognition for bundled products and services, you will hit walls. You can integrate around some gaps, but each integration adds another thing to maintain. Small manufacturers often underestimate that maintenance cost until an order breaks because a connector failed.

NetSuite starts where Katana stops. Its manufacturing module handles bills of material, work orders, routings, and production scheduling inside the same system as general ledger, accounts payable, accounts receivable, and inventory valuation. That matters when the CFO needs margin by product line, the operations lead needs material requirements planning, and the sales team needs to quote from live availability. For a small manufacturer with multiple entities, currencies, or sales channels, NetSuite's single data model is the main selling point.

That breadth has a tax. NetSuite is not a self-serve tool for most manufacturers. It usually requires a partner for configuration, data migration, and training, and the project scope can expand as departments ask for their own workflows. The licensing model is subscription-based and priced by users, modules, and transaction volume, so costs vary significantly by deployment size. A small manufacturer may find that the software can do almost anything, but the effort to configure and govern it is the real constraint.

Katana's pricing is more transparent and lower at entry. NetSuite pricing is quote-based and usually higher, often with an annual contract and implementation fees on top. But comparing license costs alone is misleading. The useful comparison is total cost of ownership: license, implementation partner, internal project time, integrations, ongoing admin, and the cost of working around missing features. A cheaper tool that forces your team into spreadsheets can cost more than a suite that fits.

Here is a simple way to think about fit. Choose Katana if manufacturing is relatively simple, you have a modest production team, you sell online or through a small distributor network, and you want to run production without hiring an ERP administrator. Choose NetSuite if manufacturing is the core of a growing business, you need financials and operations in one place, you have multi-channel or multi-entity complexity, and you are ready to pay for a partner-led implementation. The line is not size alone. It is process complexity and reporting requirements.

I have seen small manufacturers buy NetSuite too early because they feared outgrowing Katana. That fear is real, but it is not a reason to skip the stage where a simpler system teaches you how your BOMs, routings, and inventory actually behave. I have also seen Katana stretched past its limits, with spreadsheets reappearing for costing and capacity planning. Pick the system that matches the next two years, not the next ten. You can migrate later, but you cannot get back the time spent fighting a tool that was wrong on day one.

The practical test is a process review. Map how a sales order becomes a work order, how material moves, how scrap is recorded, and how the month-end close produces margin by product. Then ask which system can run that process with the fewest manual steps and the cleanest audit trail. If Katana can, buy it. If NetSuite is the only one that can, budget for the implementation, not just the subscription.
