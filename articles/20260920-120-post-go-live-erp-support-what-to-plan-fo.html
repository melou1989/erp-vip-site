Post Go-Live ERP Support: What to Plan For

PUBLISHED September 20, 2026 · GUIDE / Support Planning

Go-live is a date, not a finish line. The Monday after cutover, the questions change: why did this invoice fail validation, why is the warehouse scanner not posting receipts, why does the trial balance not match the legacy close. If you haven't named who owns those questions, the implementation team is already halfway out the door. Post go-live support is its own project, with its own budget, roles, and exit criteria.

Hypercare is the bridge between project and run. It usually means heightened coverage—daily triage, faster response, more hands on deck—but the useful part is not the label. It's the exit criteria. Define what must be true before hypercare ends: backlog under a certain threshold, critical integrations stable through a full business cycle, month-end close completed without manual workarounds, and knowledge transferred to permanent staff. Without that, hypercare becomes an expensive habit.

A shared inbox is not a support model. You need a service desk that captures ERP incidents and requests separately, with severity definitions tied to business impact. A broken payroll posting is not the same as a request for a new cost center. Triage should route to functional, technical, integration, or security queues. If every ticket goes to the same senior analyst, your best people become a bottleneck and your backlog grows in the dark.

SLAs should match the business calendar, not just the clock. A severity-one issue at 2 a.m. on a normal Tuesday may be less urgent than a report failure on the morning of close. Partner SLAs often cover response and restoration, but not root-cause analysis or permanent fixes. Read the contract for what happens when the fix requires a vendor patch, a configuration change, or a custom code correction. Those paths have different owners.

The implementation partner knows your system better than your team does on day one. That advantage disappears if they leave without documentation. Plan reverse shadowing: your analysts run the fix while the partner watches, then the partner steps back. Record configuration decisions, integration maps, security role design, and report logic. This is boring work. It also determines whether you can hire a new AP analyst without calling the SI.

Cloud ERP means you don't own the upgrade calendar. SAP, Oracle, Microsoft, Workday, and others push updates on their own cadence, whether major releases or continuous changes. Every release can affect integrations, reports, custom extensions, and role assignments. Someone must own regression testing for the processes that matter: order-to-cash, procure-to-pay, record-to-report, and payroll if it's in scope. Skipping this because it's just a minor update is how a small change breaks month-end.

Most post go-live fires start at the edges. Bank feeds stop, tax engines reject files, EDI partners send malformed orders, warehouse devices lose connectivity, CRM records duplicate. Keep an integration inventory with owners, monitoring, and replay procedures. For data, agree on who can fix records and how fixes are logged. Ad hoc SQL updates by well-meaning analysts create audit findings and break referential integrity. If a correction is common, fix the process or the configuration, not the row.

The first few closes are the real acceptance test. Build a close checklist that mirrors the new system, not the old one. Watch for manual journal entries that should be automated, unposted subledger items, reconciliation differences, and report totals that don't tie. The CFO needs to know whether the numbers are trustworthy. That's a support issue, not just an accounting issue.

Post go-live support is opex, and it rarely fits neatly in the project budget. Pricing varies significantly by vendor, deployment size, module mix, and whether support comes from the vendor, the implementation partner, or a managed services provider. Third-party support can reduce cost for mature SAP and Oracle estates, but it changes your access to patches and upgrades. Decide what you're buying: insurance, expertise, or both.

Run a weekly support review for the first several months, then keep a lighter monthly rhythm. Track ticket volume by module, backlog aging, reopened tickets, change success rate, close duration, and integration failures. Metrics are not for blaming the team. They show where training didn't stick, where a process is broken, and where you need to hire or renegotiate a contract.

Vendor support and partner support are not interchangeable. The vendor owns product defects and patches. The partner usually owns configuration, extensions, and process advice. Your internal team owns adoption, data quality, and business process discipline. When an issue lands in the wrong queue, days disappear. Write an escalation map with names and contacts before go-live, not after the first payroll failure.

Don't let post go-live support drift forever. Set a review point when hypercare ends, then another when the support model moves to steady state. Retire temporary workarounds. Update training. Close the war room. The goal is not zero tickets; it's a team that can handle normal tickets without a partner sitting in every call.
