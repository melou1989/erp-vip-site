How to Choose an ERP Implementation Partner: A Practical Guide

PUBLISHED September 14, 2026 · GUIDE / Selection

The software demo is the least predictive thing about your ERP project. Two manufacturers buy the same product in the same year, and one is closing the books in it while the other is on its third go-live date. The difference is almost never the module list. It is the people doing the configuration, the decisions made in a workshop on a Thursday afternoon, the state of the legacy data, and whether anyone bothered to test the bank file before the cutover weekend. Buyers spend months comparing vendors and a week picking the partner who determines whether any of it works. That ratio is backwards.

Start by being precise about who you are actually hiring. For most mid-market deals, you do not contract implementation with SAP, Oracle, Microsoft, or Infor directly; you contract with a reseller, value-added reseller, or systems integrator. Even the tier-one vendors who run their own consulting arms hand work to subcontractors. Ask flat out which legal entity signs the statement of work, who employs the people who will show up on Monday morning, and whether any of the delivery is subcontracted again. If the answer is vague, that is the answer.

Reference calls are the most abused part of this process. Partners hand you three customers who adore them, and those calls tell you almost nothing. Ask for references that match your shape: same industry, similar revenue band, same modules, same deployment model, and a similar number of legal entities. Then ask for one that is at least a year past go-live. Everyone is happy the week after cutover. The useful questions are boring ones. What did the change orders total against the original estimate? Who escalated when things went sideways, and how fast did they respond? Would you hire them for phase two? A reference who says yes to all three without pausing is either lucky or being diplomatic.

What you are buying is hours from specific humans, not a brand. The partner's global footprint is a sales slide; the named team is the product. Get names, roles, allocation percentages, and CVs before you sign anything, and then ask the uncomfortable question: what happens if the solution architect leaves in month two? They will, sometimes. Put a substitution clause in the contract requiring replacements of equivalent experience and your written approval. Some firms agree immediately. Some stall, and the stalling tells you how the rest of the project will go.

Onshore, offshore, and nearshore mixes are neither good nor bad on their own. What matters is where each activity sits. Who runs the fit-gap workshops? Who writes the functional specs? Who configures? Who is awake at 2 a.m. during cutover? Time-zone overlap matters more than rate cards when you are triaging defects in the last three weeks before go-live. A well-designed blend with a senior onshore lead and a lower-cost build team can work. It breaks when the person who understood your requirements is three hand-offs away from the person doing the configuration.

Pricing models deserve more scrutiny than they usually get. Fixed-fee sounds safe until you read the scope document, which is typically written narrowly enough that everything you assumed was included arrives later as a change order. Straight time and materials shifts all the risk to you. Capped T&M with a clearly defined change-order process is often the most honest structure available. Costs vary significantly by vendor, deployment size, module count, data quality, and geography, so treat any dollar figure produced before a blueprint phase with suspicion.

Do not sign a full implementation contract based on an estimate from the sales cycle. Pay for a discovery or blueprint phase first, with real deliverables: process maps, a fit-gap list, a data migration plan, an integration inventory, and a re-estimate produced by the people who will do the work. Fix the price on that phase. If the partner turns out to be wrong for you, you have spent a small fraction of the budget learning it. If they are right, you have bought the plan you will execute against, and you now have a far better basis for negotiating the rest.

Data migration and integrations are where project budgets go to die, and they are almost always underestimated. Ask how many mock migrations are included, who writes the extract from the legacy system (usually you, and your team may not have the skills), who reconciles opening balances, and who tests the interfaces with payroll, the warehouse system, the bank files, and whatever CRM sales refuses to give up. This work is unglamorous, it cannot be compressed much, and it is the single most common reason a go-live slips a quarter.

Ask how the partner actually runs projects. SAP Activate, Microsoft's Success by Design, Oracle's unified method, NetSuite's SuiteSuccess — most partners claim alignment with one of them. What you want to see is whether the deliverables map to real gates with real sign-offs, or whether the methodology is a slide template with the vendor's logo on the corner. A partner that cannot show you a project plan from a recent engagement, with the actual dates and the actual slippage, is selling you a story.

Training and change management get treated as optional line items and then become the reason people keep working around the new system. Ask who writes the training material, whether it is train-the-trainer or end-user sessions, whether the consultants who configured the system are still around during hypercare, and whether anyone will be on the floor during your first month-end close. A system that is technically live and functionally ignored is a failed project, just a slower and more expensive one.

Be suspicious of a partner who agrees with everything in workshops. You are paying for judgment, including being told that your chart of accounts will cause problems in consolidation and that the approval workflow you sketched has eleven steps too many. Ask how disagreements escalate and who the partner's executive sponsor is — a specific person who answers the phone when both project managers have run out of options. If the escalation path ends at the account manager, you do not have one.

The contract should cover a handful of things that buyers routinely forget. Who owns the intellectual property in custom code. Whether acceptance criteria are tied to outcomes, such as a completed month-end close with a clean audit trail, rather than a vague statement that the system is available. How long the warranty and hypercare periods run. Whether there is a transition clause so you are not held hostage by the only firm that understands your configuration. And who supports you afterward — ask to meet the support team, because the implementation consultants are often already booked on someone else's kickoff.

Boutique firms and large integrators fail differently. A boutique gives you senior people with deep niche knowledge and no bench to absorb a resignation or an acquisition. A large firm gives you process, reach, and more leverage when something goes badly wrong, at the cost of juniors on the ground and a change-order machine. The right answer depends on your internal capability. If nobody on your side has run a project of this size, buy the hand-holding, even at a premium.

One thing you cannot outsource: the partner cannot be your decision-maker. Someone internal — a project manager or product owner with real authority and most of their calendar cleared — has to own scope, sign off on design choices, and manage the business subject matter experts. Partners fill vacuums, and a vacuum on the client side produces a system configured around whatever the lead consultant did at their last customer. That is how you end up with a technically successful implementation that nobody in operations wants to use.

If you cannot get straight answers about references, named staff, and change-order history before the contract is signed, you will not get them afterward.
