ERP Change Management: How to Get Real Employee Buy-In

PUBLISHED September 15, 2026 · GUIDE / Change Management

Most ERP rollouts don't fail because the software can't handle the work. They fail because the people doing the work decide the old way is better, and nothing in the project plan stops them. The system goes live, the license gets paid, and a year later somebody in accounts payable is still keeping a private spreadsheet because "the new one takes longer."

You can spot it early if you know what to look for. Users who export every report to Excel before they start reading it. A "temporary" extension of legacy system access past the cutover date. Month-end close run in both systems, just to compare. Nobody announces any of this. It accumulates.

Treating change management as a communications workstream — a few town halls, a newsletter, an e-learning module in the final week — produces exactly the adoption numbers you'd expect. Buy-in isn't a message. It's what happens when people have a hand in decisions they'll have to live with for years.

SAP calls this fit-to-standard in its Activate methodology; other vendors run the same exercise under different names. Business users sit in a room and walk through how the system handles order-to-cash or procure-to-pay out of the box, then argue about the gaps. The arguing is the point. Someone who has personally pushed back on a screen design and lost the argument is far more likely to use it than someone who first sees it in a training session.

Pick the skeptics first. The enthusiastic early adopter will tell you the project is going well whether or not it is. The warehouse supervisor who has run the same process for fourteen years and thinks your timeline is fantasy is the person whose opinion actually moves the room. Give that person the earliest, ugliest look at the system and a real channel to raise problems. If they come around, they'll convert more colleagues than any internal campaign.

Every major vendor expects a super-user or key-user network — SAP's business process owners, Oracle's super users, NetSuite's champions, whatever the deck calls them. The failure mode is identical across all of them: people get the title, sit through a two-day overview, and go back to their day job. Then go-live arrives and the help desk is the only support channel. If you want that network to function, it has to be a real allocation of hours, with the backlog taken off those people elsewhere.

Middle managers are the group most likely to quietly kill a rollout, and the least likely to appear in a change plan. Their compensation is usually tied to metrics the new system will temporarily make worse — slower cycle times, more exceptions, an unfamiliar approval path. If you don't rework those targets before go-live, you're asking a manager to take a personal financial hit for the project's benefit. Most won't.

Data migration is where goodwill goes to die. The plan typically assumes business users will clean supplier records, customer masters, and open items in the gaps between their actual jobs. They won't, or they'll do it badly at 9pm on a Friday. If the data work isn't resourced as a real task with real hours — ideally with people temporarily relieved of other duties — expect duplicate vendors, orphaned cost centers, and a reporting layer nobody trusts.

Training too early is a quiet killer. Sessions run two months before go-live are forgotten by launch, and people asked to retrain get resentful about it. Keep sessions short, role-based, and close to cutover. A sandbox configured the way your system will actually be configured beats a vendor demo environment every time. In-app guidance — Oracle Guided Learning, the help panes in Dynamics 365, comparable tools elsewhere — sees more use than a PDF manual because it appears at the moment of confusion rather than weeks before it.

Don't solicit feedback you have no intention of acting on. Running a workshop, collecting forty items, and filing them under "phase two" costs more credibility than never asking at all. If something is out of scope, say so in the room and give the reason. People accept no. They don't accept silence.

The useful communication is boring and specific. Not a quarterly all-hands about digital transformation, but a short note saying what changes for one team this week, what they need to do about it, and who to ask. If your comms plan can't be rewritten for a single department, it isn't a comms plan. It's a press release.

Executives also need to hear the honest version. Every ERP cutover costs productivity for a period — people are slower on unfamiliar screens, and exceptions take longer to resolve. If leadership has been promised flat output from week one, that pressure lands on frontline users as overtime and blame, and the system gets the reputation for it. Setting the expectation that the first close will be slow buys you the goodwill to get the second one right.

Hypercare is where the system's reputation gets set. The first weeks after go-live determine whether users trust it. Somebody needs to be physically near the people doing the work — the receiving dock, the AP desk, the shop floor — not parked on a bridge call. An escalation path that takes three days to answer a question about a blocked invoice teaches users to route around the system, and once they've learned that, they don't unlearn it.

Measure adoption with usage data, not survey scores. Transaction volumes in the new system against historical volumes in the old one. Logins by role. Records created in the first hour of a shift. Satisfaction surveys at go-live are close to useless, because people are being polite to the person standing in front of them. Usage data doesn't have manners.

Watch the people who know the old processes best. They're the most exposed during a transition, since their expertise is the thing being retired, and some respond by leaving. Losing a twenty-year AP lead mid-project costs more than most of the software decisions on the plan. The counterweight is new hires who onboard directly onto the new system. They have no old habits to defend, and they'll tell you plainly which steps make no sense.

None of this is a phase with a start and end date. The project team disbands, and the process owners who inherit the system either keep improving it or let it drift back toward the old shape. Buy-in is really just whether those people feel ownership of it. If you can't name the three users most likely to undermine your rollout, the stakeholder analysis isn't finished.
