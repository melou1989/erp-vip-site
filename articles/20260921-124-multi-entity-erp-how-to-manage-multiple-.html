Multi-Entity ERP: How to Manage Multiple Subsidiaries

PUBLISHED September 21, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Architecture

A group with a US parent, a German GmbH, a Singapore trading entity and a Brazilian LTDA rarely runs one ERP. What it usually runs is one system at headquarters, two or three local systems chosen by whoever was on the ground at the time, and a consolidation spreadsheet that one person in group finance understands and nobody else can audit. Multi-entity ERP is the attempt to fix that, and it is harder than the vendor brochures suggest.

There are two architectures, and most groups pick between them badly. The first is a single instance with many legal entities: NetSuite OneWorld, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Finance, Sage Intacct, Acumatica, Oracle Fusion Cloud Financials and SAP S/4HANA all let you run separate statutory books inside one system with intercompany automation. The second is two-tier — headquarters on S/4HANA or Oracle, subsidiaries on Business One, Business Central or a local package, with consolidation sitting above everything. Single instance gives you cleaner intercompany and worse local fit. Two-tier gives you the reverse, plus a data latency problem that grows every year.

At the transaction level, multi-entity means this: a sale from the German entity to the US entity creates receivables in one set of books and payables in another. The posting is the easy part. The matching is not. If Germany books intercompany revenue on the 28th and the US books the expense in the following period, your eliminations go out of balance and somebody spends close week chasing a difference that is really just timing. Intercompany matching workflows — auto-created counterparty documents, tolerance thresholds, an exception queue someone actually reviews — matter far more than a consolidated dashboard that looks good in a demo.

Then there is the question of where consolidation happens. ERP-native consolidation, whether that is the general ledger consolidation features in Dynamics 365, NetSuite's consolidated reporting or Sage Intacct's entity rollups, handles a simple structure well: one chart of accounts, one reporting currency, one GAAP, wholly owned subsidiaries. Add minority interest, a step acquisition, an equity-method joint venture or a second reporting standard and those modules start to strain. That is the point where groups move consolidation up a layer — SAP S/4HANA Group Reporting, Oracle Financial Consolidation and Close, OneStream, CCH Tagetik — because those tools are built around ownership hierarchies, group-level eliminations, currency translation reserve and non-controlling interest rather than around journal entries.

Chart of accounts design is where multi-entity projects quietly fail. Force one global chart on every entity and local accountants spend their month writing reclassification journals so the statutory filing looks right. Let every entity keep its own chart and group reporting depends on a mapping table with a named owner — and that mapping table will be wrong by the third quarter, because someone added a GL account in Manila and nobody told corporate. Whichever way you go, assign ownership of the chart and the mapping. Unassigned master data governance is the single most common reason consolidation takes three weeks instead of five days.

Multi-book accounting is table stakes now, and it is one of the few places where the mid-market vendors genuinely compete. NetSuite, Sage Intacct, Dynamics 365 Finance and others support parallel ledgers so you can carry IFRS, local GAAP and tax-basis books simultaneously. The differences that actually hurt are revenue recognition timing, lease accounting under IFRS 16 versus ASC 842, inventory valuation method and the deferred tax that falls out of all of it. Running parallel books doubles reconciliation work, and it is still better than retrofitting a local ledger after an audit finding.

Local compliance is the constraint that breaks the single-instance argument. Italy's SDI, Mexico's CFDI, Brazil's Nota Fiscal, India's GST e-invoicing, SAF-T filings in Portugal and Poland — global vendors certify these per country, but certifications land on different release cadences, and a subsidiary three versions behind loses its compliance. This is why so many groups tier deliberately: keep the local system where the statutory mandate is hardest to satisfy from a distance, and accept the integration cost.

Ownership structure is the next thing that breaks naive setups. Wholly owned subsidiaries are easy. An 80% holding with non-controlling interest is not. Neither is a mid-year acquisition that needs pre- and post-acquisition periods, an entity in a hyperinflationary economy requiring restatement under IAS 29 before translation, or a cumulative translation adjustment that has to stay in other comprehensive income rather than leaking into the P&L.

One practical instruction for evaluation. In vendor demos, refuse to let them run their own intercompany scenario. Bring your actual group structure — the holding company, the dormant entities, the one subsidiary that invoices in three currencies — and ask where the elimination entries get booked, at which level, and who approves them. Ask what happens when a new entity is added mid-year: chart mapping, currency, tax registration, opening balances, and how long that setup really takes. Ask who can change the chart of accounts and what stops them. If the answer is that the system handles all of it automatically, ask to see the screen where the elimination entry lands.
