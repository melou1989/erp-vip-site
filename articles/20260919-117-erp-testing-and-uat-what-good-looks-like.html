ERP Testing and UAT: What Good Looks Like

PUBLISHED September 19, 2026 · GUIDE / Testing Strategy

Most ERP testing problems start long before anyone opens a test script. If the business process design is vague, UAT becomes a debate about what the system should do rather than whether it does it. Good testing begins with process owners writing scenarios that reflect how they actually close the books, buy materials, ship orders, or pay employees. Those scenarios should trace back to the fit-gap decisions made during design.

UAT is not a second round of unit testing. Unit testing proves a configuration works in isolation. Integration testing proves modules and external systems exchange data correctly. UAT proves the business can run its operations with the new system. If consultants are driving the keyboard while users watch, you are doing a demo, not acceptance testing.

The right participants matter more than the script count. A UAT team made of department managers who haven't touched a purchase order in years will miss the edge cases that break daily work. Bring in the AP clerk who handles three-way match exceptions, the warehouse lead who deals with returns, the controller who reconciles intercompany. Give them authority to raise defects and time to test.

Test data is where many ERP projects quietly fail. Development data is too clean. Production data is too sensitive. You need masked, production-like data that includes partial receipts, credit memos, backorders, multi-currency invoices, tax exemptions, and historical balances. Data migration tests should reconcile opening balances to the legacy system, not just confirm that rows loaded.

Integrations deserve their own test track. Bank feeds, payroll, CRM, WMS, EDI, tax engines, and expense tools rarely fail in the middle of a clean transaction. They fail on timeouts, duplicate messages, rejected files, and partial failures. Test with real endpoints or realistic stubs, and test what happens when the other system is down or returns an error.

Security and controls often get pushed to the end. That is backward. During UAT, test segregation of duties, approval thresholds, field-level access, and audit trails. Can a procurement user create a vendor and pay it? Can a plant manager approve a purchase order above limit? Can finance see salary data? Auditors will ask, and fixing roles after go-live is painful.

Reports and analytics are part of the system, not an afterthought. If the new ERP cannot produce the margin report, the aging report, or the operational dashboard the business relies on, users will keep spreadsheets alive. Test report layouts, drill-downs, filters, and reconciliation to source transactions. A report that totals correctly but cannot explain a variance is not finished.

Automation helps, but it does not replace human judgment. Tools like Tricentis, Worksoft, Opkey, and Panaya can regression-test repetitive flows after a fix or patch. They are useful for order-to-cash, procure-to-pay, and record-to-report cycles. They will not tell you whether an approval workflow makes sense for a regional controller. Use automation for repetition; use people for judgment.

Exit criteria should be written before UAT starts. Define defect severity, how many open critical defects are tolerable, who can accept a workaround, and what evidence counts as sign-off. 'No critical defects' sounds clear until someone argues about whether a broken tax calculation is critical. Agree on severity definitions and owners early.

Test the cutover, not just the software. A conference room pilot or day-in-the-life simulation should run the sequence of activities that happen during go-live: data load, validation, reconciliation, user access checks, first transactions, first close tasks. Many failures happen because the plan assumed a clean handoff between teams that never practiced together.

Good UAT leaves a trail. Defect logs, retest results, sign-off forms, and open items with owners and dates. That trail matters during go-live when everyone is tired and someone asks whether a known issue was accepted. It also matters after go-live when the business wants to improve a process. Without evidence, every argument restarts from memory.

The uncomfortable truth is that ERP testing is risk management. It costs time and pulls people away from their day jobs. Skipping it does not remove the risk; it moves the risk to production, where invoices fail, inventory is wrong, and month-end takes longer. Good testing is not about passing scripts. It is about proving the business can operate.
