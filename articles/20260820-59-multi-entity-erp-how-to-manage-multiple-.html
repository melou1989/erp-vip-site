Multi-Entity ERP: How to Manage Multiple Subsidiaries

PUBLISHED August 20, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Strategy

Most growing companies hit the same wall: their org chart no longer matches the legal structure. You buy a subsidiary, open a branch in another country, or hold entities for tax reasons, and suddenly the ERP that worked for one company is creaking under the weight of five. The typical response is to demand 'multi-entity' support, but that phrase covers a wide spectrum of capabilities, and the gap between what vendors demo and what your controllers will actually live with is where projects go to die.

At its core, multi-entity ERP means two things: each legal entity gets its own books, and the system can still produce consolidated numbers that eliminate intercompany transactions. Those two requirements fight each other. If every subsidiary runs a fully separate ledger with its own chart of accounts, you'll spend every month-end manually mapping accounts and chasing transfer prices. If everything is forced into one shared set of codes, your local controllers lose the freedom they need for country-specific tax treatment. Serious platforms—SAP S/4HANA, Oracle Fusion, NetSuite—approach this differently. NetSuite, for instance, gives you one database with subsidiary records that can own their own master data, but allows shared items and customers. SAP and Oracle tend to use legal-entity segmentation within a unified chart. There is no right answer, only the question of how much local autonomy you're willing to trade for consolidation speed.

The procurement mistake I see most often is when an evaluation team focuses on consolidation reports and intercompany eliminations, then forgets that subsidiaries have different fiscal calendars, VAT rules, and even payment practices. One of your German entities can't just adopt the U.S. parent's month-end close schedule. Multi-entity ERPs solve that with balancing segments and conversion rules, but only if you've defined the intercompany workflows in advance. Who issues the intercompany invoice? What currency does it get settled in? What happens when the upstream entity and downstream entity record the same transaction on different dates? Untrained demos make these look trivial. They aren't. If you don't lock down the operating model first, the software will faithfully preserve your inconsistent processes.

Pricing varies significantly by vendor and deployment size, but a bigger long-term cost is the chart of accounts design and data ownership model. If you let each subsidiary maintain its own item master and supplier list, consolidation becomes a data-cleansing exercise that never ends. But forcing a single global warehouse of records on, say, a distribution company in Brazil and a SaaS service in Singapore can be just as wrong. The vendors that do this well give you tiered control—some fields are globally governed, others are entity-local. That is the actual feature to look for, not a sexy memo about 'real-time consolidation.' Ask the vendor to show you how a transaction entered in a foreign subsidiary's local ledger flows into a parent-level eliminations report, including the FX revaluation. Then ask what happens when one legal entity buys from another. If the answer is 'push a button and it's automated,' walk away. It's never that simple.
