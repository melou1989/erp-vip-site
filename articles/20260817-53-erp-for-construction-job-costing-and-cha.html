ERP for Construction: Job Costing and Change Orders Are the Real Test

PUBLISHED August 17, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Cost Management

Construction ERP isn't just accounting with extra fields for jobs. The two areas that actually separate a construction ERP from a generic ERP are job costing and change orders. They force the system to deal with the messy reality of projects, where a single decision can ripple through the budget, the schedule, the subcontractor, and the client invoice all at once.

Job costing at its core means comparing estimated costs against actual costs for labor, material, equipment, and subcontractors. Most systems can do that. The trouble starts with how costs get captured. If the system doesn't integrate with field time sheets, material receipts, and vendor invoices, your job cost report is just a rearview mirror. The vendors that do this well push you to adopt a project-specific cost code structure across every job — and that is more of a management discipline than a software feature.

Change orders are the harder problem. A change order isn't a simple contract adjustment; it impacts several moving parts: the forecasted cost of the job, the budget line items, the billing schedule, even the retention amount held by the client. A lot of ERPs treat a change order as a sales order or a revenue entry, losing the connection to the extra costs you already incurred getting the work done. The systems built for construction model the full workflow: the prime contract change, the subcontractor change, the cost budget revision, the invoice impact. Without that, you end up with delayed billing or unbilled costs you carry for months.

Honestly, the demo is where you should push hard. Ask each vendor to walk through a specific scenario: a client approves a change order for additional work, but you've already booked the subcontractor's invoice for that work. Does the system link the approved revenue to that subcontractor cost? Does the job cost report update automatically? Or do you need to manually reclassify entries to make the numbers match? In practice, many construction companies discover these gaps only after go-live, when the accounting team is doing backflips to reconcile the change order log against the general ledger.

Also remember that change orders that get rejected still cost money. A real construction ERP lets you track pending changes separately from approved ones, and includes the authorized-but-unapproved work in your internal cost forecast without billing it yet. That is often missing from general-purpose ERPs. If the system can't separate the approved change from the pending change, you'll never know your real margin until the job is closed. Ask for a reference with a messy change order history — a partial approval, a disputed subcontractor change, a retention clause. See if the job cost report stays in sync without manual journal entries. That will tell you more than any feature checklist.

The construction ERP market reflects this split. There are systems purpose-built for project accounting, like Sage 300 Construction and Real Estate, Viewpoint Vista (now Trimble), and CMiC, alongside general ERP platforms that offer a construction module bolted on. The difference shows up exactly here, in the change order lifecycle. Pricing varies significantly by vendor and deployment size, and implementation timeframes depend on whether you're willing to standardize your cost structure. But the software is never the real cost. The real cost is what you lose when the job cost report doesn't reflect what the change order actually did to the job.
