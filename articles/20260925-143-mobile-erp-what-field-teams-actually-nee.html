Mobile ERP: What Field Teams Actually Need

PUBLISHED September 25, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Field Operations

Ask a service technician what she wants from software and you won't hear the word "ERP." She wants the work order to be correct when she opens it, the part to be on the truck when she reaches for it, and the job to close out before she drives home. Mobile ERP is really the discipline of making back-office records behave that way on a five-inch screen in a basement mechanical room.

Offline is not a checkbox. Almost every mobile ERP demo happens on office Wi-Fi, which is the one place the product will never be used. Reading data offline is easy — cache the work order, the asset history, the parts list. Writing offline is where projects die. A technician closes a job in a dead zone, consumes three parts, captures a signature, books two hours of labor. The sync queue has to hold all of that, then reconcile it against a warehouse that shipped the same part to a different job while she was offline. Vendors handle the conflict differently: some flag it and route it to a dispatcher, some quietly go last-write-wins field by field, some just drop the record. Ask which, and make them show you.

"ERP on a phone" is the wrong brief. The transaction set a field crew needs is narrow: look up an asset and its service history, accept or decline a work order, mark arrival and departure, consume parts and record returns, capture meter readings, notes, photos and a customer signature, then close. Purchasing, the general ledger, and MRP runs belong on a laptop. Teams that try to port the whole menu get an app nobody opens.

Plenty of what vendors market as a mobile app is a responsive web page in a native shell. That is fine for approving a requisition. It falls over the moment the camera has to read a lot or serial barcode in poor light, or the app has to talk to a Bluetooth torque wrench, label printer, or thermal receipt printer on the truck. Native apps from SAP, Microsoft, IFS and ServiceNow reach those device APIs. Browser-based ERP modules mostly don't, and no amount of CSS fixes that.

The van is a warehouse with no walls, and that is where mobile ERP earns or loses its money. Trunk stock accuracy drifts because issuing happens on paper and consuming happens from memory. If the app scans the part at pick and scans it again at install, the warehouse stops arguing with the technician about whether the part was ever loaded. Cycle counting from a phone during downtime, and raising a replenishment request without a phone call, closes the loop. Returns matter as much as issues — cores, unused parts, warranty swaps — and most mobile rollouts forget them until the first month-end variance.

Time capture is the politically loaded part. Field leadership wants travel time separated from wrench time, because that ratio is where margin hides. Geofenced auto-arrival is genuinely useful and technicians generally accept it when it saves them paperwork. Continuous location tracking on a personal phone is a different conversation, and in unionized or heavily regulated environments it will end in a grievance. Decide the policy before the app decides it for you.

Shared devices are common in the field and awkward in most ERP mobile apps. A truck-mounted tablet used by three techs across two shifts needs fast user switching, per-user PINs, and no bleed of one person's time entries or pay data into the next login. Add MDM for remote wipe, and think about batteries honestly: GPS, camera use, and background sync will kill a consumer phone before the end of a long shift unless charging lives in the cab.

Design for the worst signal, not the average one. Rural routes, steel-framed warehouses, hospital sub-basements, and elevator shafts are the normal case, not the edge case. That means small payloads, deferred photo and attachment upload over Wi-Fi, and a sync engine that tells the user plainly what is still pending rather than showing a green checkmark over a stack of unsent records. If the ERP itself sits behind a VPN on-premise, fix that first; no mobile client outruns a slow backend.

Then there is the commercial side, which varies significantly by vendor and deployment size. Some ERP licenses include a fixed number of mobile users, some price per named user, some per concurrent user, and several sell mobile as a separate SKU per app per month. Watch the overlap: many buyers end up owning a dedicated field service management product next to the ERP, and the integration between the two quietly becomes the largest line item in the program.

For a pilot, pick one workflow and one crew. Work order close-out is the right one — it touches parts, labor, signature, and customer notification all at once. Run it for a month and count real things: how often the dispatcher re-keys information that already exists in the field, how many sync conflicts land in the queue, how many days close-out takes versus the paper process. A scored feature matrix from four vendors will tell you less than that spreadsheet.

One thing to plan for, because it always happens: the mobile app is the harshest audit your master data will ever get. Every duplicate asset record, every part with a description but no unit of measure, every customer site whose address won't geocode — the field finds all of it within the first couple of weeks, and they find it loudly. Budget for the cleanup. It is not in the license cost.
