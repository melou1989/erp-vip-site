How to Budget for an ERP Implementation: A Practical Guide

PUBLISHED August 17, 2026 · GUIDE / Budgeting

The price quote you get from an ERP vendor is usually just the entry ticket. It covers the software licenses or subscriptions and a block of implementation consulting hours, but the real cost of doing an ERP project lives in the work your own team has to do, the data you have to clean, and the systems that need to hook into this new one. If you treat the vendor's proposal as your budget, you'll miss half the cost.

Start with the obvious lines: license or subscription fees, annual maintenance, implementation partner fees, and hardware or cloud infrastructure. Then add the things smart CFOs ask about: data migration, report rewrite, integration with existing applications, and 'testing'—which is a polite word for the weeks your people spend clicking through edge cases. On most projects, the internal project team's time exceeds what the vendor charges for consulting, especially if the finance team is still expected to run month-end close while helping configure the new system.

Here's where it gets messy. Change management and training aren't a line item on most quotes, but they're the difference between go-live and a train wreck. You'll also eat lost productivity for several months, and you may need to hire temps for order entry or pay overtime to keep up with backlog. The project will last longer than your longest guess, and the external consultants will be happy to bill you for every extra week. A budget that doesn't include a human cost for change management is a budget that pretends implementation is a technical task.

When comparing vendor proposals, ask them to write down what's excluded. 'We'll need to scope that separately' is a polite way of saying 'there's more money coming.' If your internal people have to cover the gap, that's not an IT budget problem—it's a business problem. The cheapest license quote often translates into the most expensive project, because the cost just gets shifted to your own team's unbilled hours. Budgeting for ERP is really about being honest about what your organization is willing to spend in time, attention, and broken old habits—not just what it costs to acquire software.
