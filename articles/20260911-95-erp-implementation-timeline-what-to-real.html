ERP Implementation Timeline: What to Realistically Expect

PUBLISHED September 11, 2026 · GUIDE / Strategy

The timeline you get during the sales cycle and the timeline you actually live through are two different documents. The first one ends at go-live and assumes every decision arrives on schedule, every integration works as documented, and your data is cleaner than it is. The second one includes the weeks nobody budgeted for reconciling the item master and arguing about whether the old approval workflow still matters.

Most buyers start the clock at contract signature. That is convenient for the vendor and misleading for you. Selection, business case, process mapping, and the internal alignment work all happen before the clock starts, and they determine how fast the rest moves. A company that walks into kickoff with documented processes and a named process owner per area will move through design in a fraction of the time of one that is still debating who owns procurement.

Design and fit-gap is where schedule optimism first meets friction. Consultants run workshops, map your current state against the new system's standard flows, and log the gaps. The gaps are not the problem. The problem is the parked decisions — the fields nobody wants to give up, the report that three people rely on, the approval step that exists because of an auditor's comment in 2016. Every unresolved question leaves the workshop and comes back during user acceptance testing, usually at the worst possible moment. Executive sponsors who can settle disputes in a week rather than a month compress this phase more than any technical work.

Data migration is the long pole, and it is almost always understaffed because it is unglamorous. Chart of accounts redesign, customer and vendor de-duplication, open purchase orders, fixed asset registers, historical balances that need to tie to the old system. This work cannot be fully parallelized — you need the target data model before you can map, and you need the mappings before you can load test. Companies that start extracting and profiling legacy data during selection, rather than after kickoff, buy themselves real time later.

Configuration versus customization deserves its own conversation about schedule. Configuration is setting up the system the way it was designed to be set up. Customization is changing the code or building extensions, and every extension has to be tested, documented, and re-tested through every upgrade for the life of the system. A single custom approval engine can add weeks to the build phase and resurface every time the vendor pushes a release. The projects that finish close to plan tend to be the ones that made peace with changing a process rather than bending the software.

Integrations are the quiet schedule killer. Payroll, warehouse management, CRM, EDI with trading partners, bank feeds, expense tools, the homegrown reporting database that finance will not release. Each one involves a third party with its own backlog, its own sandbox, and its own definition of 'urgent.' If your integration list is longer than a page, treat the integration phase as a project of its own with its own owner, not a line item buried inside the ERP build.

Testing is where timelines go to die politely. Unit testing is manageable. System integration testing exposes the gaps between modules. Then user acceptance testing arrives, and the business users who promised to be available are still doing their day jobs. UAT scripts get written late, testers run out of time, and defects get triaged into 'fix before go-live' and 'we'll live with it.' The second category is how you end up with a stabilization period that lasts a full quarter.

Training and change management get compressed into the final weeks, every time, regardless of what the plan says. It is the easiest thing to squeeze when the build is behind, and the most expensive thing to squeeze when people log in on day one and cannot complete a task they have done for a decade. Nobody blames the training plan. They blame the software. Budget the training hours early, and do not let them become the buffer for someone else's slippage.

Cutover strategy changes the shape of the timeline more than most people expect. A big-bang cutover concentrates all the risk into one weekend and gives you a clean break. A phased rollout — by module, by site, by legal entity — spreads risk but keeps legacy systems running alongside the new one, which means dual data entry, reconciliation, and a project that drags on for months longer than anyone wants. Both approaches work. Choosing badly, or deferring the choice until month eight, does not.

Go-live is not the finish line. There is hypercare, the period right after cutover when the project team sits next to the business and fixes what breaks. Then there is stabilization, when transaction volumes hit and the edge cases show up. Then there is the backlog marked 'phase two' — the reports, the automations, the workflows that were descoped to protect the date. Mature organizations plan for post-go-live support as an ongoing function, not a temporary courtesy.

What actually stretches a timeline is rarely the software. It is the number of legal entities, currencies, tax regimes, and languages in scope. It is whether you are replacing one system or five. It is manufacturing depth, regulatory requirements, and how many third parties have to cooperate. Above all, it is decision latency. A project with ten decision-makers who respond within 48 hours will beat a project with three who take two weeks, every time.

As a rough frame: a single-entity mid-market deployment on a cloud platform with standard processes and limited integration can be measured in months, though still more months than the sales deck suggests. A multi-country, multi-entity rollout with manufacturing, regulatory reporting, and a decade of legacy data will commonly run past a year and often closer to two. Anyone quoting materially less than those shapes is describing a different project than the one you actually have.

The date in the project plan is a forecast, not a commitment, and treating it as a commitment is how teams start cutting testing to protect a number on a slide. Build the plan with real slack around data, testing, and training — the three phases that always absorb the overruns — and write down who has the authority to change scope when the schedule moves. That single governance decision does more for your timeline than any tool.
