ERP for Nonprofits: Fund Accounting Explained

PUBLISHED August 14, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Fund Accounting Guide

Fund accounting is not just a way to keep the books for a nonprofit. It is a legal and practical response to how money arrives with strings attached. A donation for a new roof cannot be spent on office laptops. A federal grant might require you to track spending down to a specific program service, sometimes with the clock running on a matching-funds deadline. Commercial accounting tracks profitability by product line. Fund accounting tracks stewardship by purpose. That distinction is the reason a generic ERP often fails in a nonprofit.

When a nonprofit CFO starts evaluating ERP software, the first question is not 'does it support fund accounting?' Every vendor will say yes. The real question is how. Some ERP products, like Sage Intacct and Blackbaud Financial Edge, were built with nonprofit accounting baked into the core. Others, such as Microsoft Dynamics 365 and Oracle NetSuite, are commercial platforms that offer fund accounting through field mappings, custom dimensions, or partner add-ons. That difference becomes visible in daily use. A true fund accounting system enforces a balanced fund balance on every transaction. A workaround lets you tag a transaction with a fund code and hopes you never let the tag and the balance drift apart.

The operational strain shows up with restricted grants. A good nonprofit ERP lets you set up each grant as its own fund or project, tracks revenue when the grant is recognized, and lets you draw down expenses against the grant's available balance. It also separates temporarily restricted net assets from unrestricted ones, honoring the distinction in the statement of financial position. Many commercial ERPs can do this after extensive configuration, but the moment your controller needs to create a fund transfer from the operations budget to cover an overrun on a restricted project, a generic setup can produce journal entries that look correct and are quietly wrong. Financial reporting that does not match the FASB's net asset classes will fail an audit, regardless of how nicely the dashboard customizes.

So where does this leave an evaluation? Look for how the software handles fund balances at the transaction level, how it handles inter-fund loans, and whether the financial statements can show the three classes of net assets without a custom Excel bridge. Ask the vendor to show a restricted grant lifecycle from award to drawdown to closeout, not just a screen with checkboxes. Then talk to a nonprofit controller who has lived through an audit with that software. If the controller hesitates before answering, keep looking.
