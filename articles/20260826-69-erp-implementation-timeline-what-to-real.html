ERP Implementation Timeline: What to Realistically Expect

PUBLISHED August 26, 2026 · GUIDE / Timeline Guidance

Every ERP project starts with someone asking how long this will take. The honest answer, before any software is chosen, is that the timeline depends less on the vendor than on what you're willing to give up. If your company insists on keeping every legacy workaround, the project will stretch. If you accept the software's standard processes, it can move.

Vendors and partners typically describe a four or five phase sequence: discovery, solution design, build or configuration, testing, and go-live. For small or mid-sized deployments, that sequence is often quoted in months. For larger multinational rollouts, a year or more is normal. What none of those phase names tells you is where the real time sinks live. Discovery gets delayed because stakeholders don't agree on requirements. Configuration is straightforward until you attach custom code to compensate for a business process nobody wants to retire. Data migration is almost always the quiet killer.

Take three external factors that actually shape the calendar. First, the number of integrated systems. Each integration to an ERP—CRM, e-commerce, bank feeds, third-party logistics—needs its own mapping, tests, and error handling. Second, the quality of your master data. If your item file has duplicates or your chart of accounts is inconsistent, someone has to clean it before loading. Third, user acceptance testing. UAT is where delays become visible: users discover the software doesn't match their expectations, and the team starts a round of change requests. The vendors who give you that initial timeline assume UAT goes cleanly. In practice it never does, unless you've frozen scope early and documented decisions obsessively.

What tends to separate quick projects from long ones is not the software. NetSuite, SAP Business One, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central, Infor CloudSuite—these can all be installed and configured in comparable elapsed time if the project stays standard. The separation comes from process standardization, data cleanup, and whether executives let the implementation team make decisions without full consensus. Your realistic timeline is the one that accounts for those two weeks of UAT where the finance director decides to change the approval workflow, and the three weeks of integration testing that expose a mismatch in order documents, and the time you set aside for pilots in remote warehouses. Plan for those and you'll still be late, just less late. Or you'll finish early and everyone will pretend the original deadline was conservative.
