Signs It's Time to Replace Your Legacy ERP System

PUBLISHED September 26, 2026 · GUIDE / Checklist

Age is the worst possible reason to replace an ERP system. I've watched companies rip out a twelve-year-old system that was doing exactly what they needed and install something newer that fit the business worse. The systems that genuinely need to go announce it in specific, measurable ways — and those signals have very little to do with a version number.

The most concrete signal is vendor support. When your vendor stops shipping patches, or moves you onto extended maintenance that costs more and delivers less, you are paying a premium for a frozen product. SAP's long-announced end of mainstream maintenance for ECC in 2027 is the loudest example, but Oracle, Microsoft, Infor, and Sage have all done versions of the same thing to their older releases. Extended support is a bridge, not a destination. If your three-year roadmap is 'stay on extended support and hope,' the decision is being made for you whether you like it or not.

Next: customizations nobody can explain. Every older system accumulates them. A screen added for a customer who left in 2016. A pricing routine written by a contractor no one can reach. A report that breaks whenever the fiscal calendar shifts. The tell is that your IT team is afraid to touch core code. When applying a vendor patch means regression-testing eleven custom objects first, you are no longer running an ERP system. You are running a fork of one, and forks don't get security updates.

Then look at the shape of the workday. If closing the books means an overnight batch job, a CSV export, and three people in a conference room with a reconciliation spreadsheet, your ERP is not the system of record. Excel is. Same story with planning: if demand forecasts live in a planner's personal workbook because the ERP can't hold that granularity, you're paying license and maintenance fees for a database doing half its job.

Integration debt shows up later and costs more. Legacy systems were built in a world of point-to-point interfaces and nightly file drops. Every new SaaS tool — a CRM, a WMS, a billing platform — needs another connector, and the map of what talks to what lives in one person's head. Ask a simple question like 'what happens to a sales order after it's entered' and watch how long it takes before someone reaches for a whiteboard.

Staffing is an underrated trigger. The hiring pool for ABAP, Oracle Forms, RPG on IBM i, or COBOL is small and getting smaller. When your only realistic option is a contractor at a rate you don't control, and your own team learns the system by watching that contractor work, the cost of staying isn't just licenses. It's the inability to change anything without buying outside help.

Compliance quietly pushes this along. Older platforms often can't enforce segregation of duties in a way an auditor will accept, can't produce a clean immutable audit trail, and sit on operating system and database versions that stopped receiving security fixes years ago. You can compensate with manual controls. Manual controls don't scale, and auditors charge you for the privilege of testing them every cycle.

And then there's the business itself. A new legal entity. A new country. A shift from perpetual licenses to subscription revenue. A direct-to-consumer channel. An acquisition that brings a second chart of accounts. If each of those takes a quarter of custom development and a year of internal arguing, the ERP is now the constraint on the company. Watch for shadow systems — a standalone WMS, a departmental billing tool, a planning app somebody bought on a credit card. Those aren't side projects. They're a replacement happening in pieces, without a plan and without an owner.

Now the honest part, because most writing on this topic oversells. Old is not the same as broken. JD Edwards EnterpriseOne still gets Oracle updates. Plenty of Sage 100, Epicor, and Infor installations are stable, well understood, and cheap to run relative to their replacement. Ripping out a system that works because a vendor rebranded a product line is how a company spends two years and a serious amount of money to arrive at roughly the same place with worse process fit.

The test I'd apply is narrower than most checklists: name the three things the business must do in the next 24 months that the current system cannot do, and be specific. 'Better reporting' isn't an answer. 'Consolidate six entities into one close in under a week without the manual elimination spreadsheet' is.

If that list is short or mostly cosmetic, a reimplementation, a version upgrade, or moving a supported on-prem product to its cloud equivalent may be enough. Oracle, Infor, Epicor, and Microsoft all maintain migration paths from older releases, and those paths are usually cheaper than a greenfield selection. If the list is long and every item touches core finance or supply chain, you're already past the point where a patch closes the gap.

In practice, replacement decisions rarely start with a strategy deck. They start with an event: a failed audit finding, an acquisition with a hard integration date, a key person retiring, a hardware refresh that turns out to be a reimplementation in disguise. The companies that handle it well are the ones that spot the signal early enough to run a selection process on their own schedule instead of the vendor's.
