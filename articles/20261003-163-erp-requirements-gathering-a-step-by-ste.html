ERP Requirements Gathering: A Step-by-Step Approach

PUBLISHED October 03, 2026 · GUIDE / Requirements Process

Most ERP requirements documents are bad because they are wish lists wearing a business case. They get written after someone sees a demo, or they are copied from a previous project. Start before vendor demos. Ask what decisions must improve: days sales outstanding, close cycle, supply planning, margin visibility, compliance reporting. Requirements should describe decisions, transactions, and outputs. If a line item cannot be tied to a decision or a legal obligation, park it in a separate parking lot.

Step 0 is agreeing on scope and decision rights. Who signs off? The CFO, COO, CIO, and functional process owners need to be named. Without decision rights, requirements gathering becomes a political exercise where every department adds pet features. Form a small steering group that meets often enough to resolve conflicts. Include finance, operations, IT, and any other function inside the initial scope, such as sales, procurement, or HR. If multiple entities, currencies, or tax jurisdictions are involved, bring those people in early.

Step 1: document current-state value streams, not every task. Pick the big flows: order-to-cash, procure-to-pay, record-to-report, plan-to-produce, hire-to-retire. For each one, capture triggers, inputs, systems, handoffs, controls, and exceptions. Do this on a whiteboard with the people who actually do the work. Record volumes too: invoices per month, SKUs, entities, currencies, warehouses, users. Volumes matter more than adjectives. A 200-page process map nobody reads is worse than a one-page flow with real numbers.

Step 2: interview by role, not by department. A controller cares about close, audit trail, revenue recognition, and consolidation. An AP clerk cares about invoice matching, payment runs, and exception handling. A warehouse supervisor cares about receiving, putaway, cycle counts, and shipping. Sales operations cares about quoting, order promising, and returns. Ask what makes them stop and open Excel. That question reveals gaps faster than any checklist. Also ask what reports they run and when. Reporting requirements often drive the data model more than transaction screens do.

Step 3: turn findings into requirement statements. A useful format is: the system must allow a role to take an action on an object under a condition so that an outcome happens. For example, the system must allow AP to match a three-way PO, receipt, and invoice with tolerance overrides and an audit trail so that payment runs do not stall. Avoid requirements like must be user-friendly. Add acceptance criteria. Assign priority honestly: regulatory or legal, revenue-impacting, operational necessity, or nice-to-have. MoSCoW labels are fine if you do not label everything as must.

Step 4: separate process requirements from data requirements. Most ERP failures are data failures. Define master data for customer, vendor, item, employee, chart of accounts, cost center, BOM, and routing. Who owns each domain? What is the source system? Which fields are mandatory? How will duplicates be prevented? Data migration is not a technical afterthought; it is a requirement. Reporting requirements belong here too: statutory reports, management reports, operational reports. Financial statements, tax filings, inventory valuation, aging, backlog, and margin analysis all need owners and definitions.

Step 5: capture integration and extension requirements. List every system that must exchange data: CRM, WMS, payroll, tax engine, EDI, banks, planning tools, e-commerce, and data warehouses. For each, define direction, frequency, volume, error handling, and master data ownership. Real-time often means I do not want to wait. Challenge it. Extensions include custom fields, workflows, forms, and reports. Ask whether the process can change instead. Customizations raise upgrade costs, but refusing all customization is also wrong when it blocks legal compliance or a real competitive advantage.

Step 6: write nonfunctional requirements. These include security roles, segregation of duties, audit trails, availability, performance, browser and mobile access, data retention, localization, and accessibility. They do not demo well, and they cause pain later. Examples: month-end close cannot be impacted by overnight batch failures. Users in three countries need local language and tax formats. Auditors need read-only access to transaction history. These requirements should be testable. If you cannot write a test case for it, it is not a requirement yet.

Step 7: prioritize and validate. Put requirements in a traceability matrix with ID, source, priority, acceptance criteria, vendor response, and test case. Review with process owners and the steering group. Kill duplicates. If two departments want different approval thresholds, escalate the conflict instead of averaging the numbers. Do not send an 800-line spreadsheet to vendors and call it an RFP. Vendors will answer standard to everything. Use a focused demo script and a smaller set of critical scenarios instead.

Step 8: use scenarios in vendor demos. Give vendors sample data and a script. Whether you are evaluating SAP S/4HANA, Microsoft Dynamics 365, NetSuite, Acumatica, Infor, Epicor, or a smaller specialist, the script is the same. Ask them to show a customer order with a backordered item, partial shipment, credit hold, and tax exemption. Watch how they navigate, not just how they talk. Record whether each requirement is standard, configuration, customization, partner add-on, or not possible. Ask what happens at upgrade time.

Step 9: fit-gap during implementation. After selection, requirements become fit-gap. Consultants map requirements to standard functionality. Gaps get resolved through process change, configuration, extension, or acceptance. This is where scope creep enters. Keep the traceability matrix alive. Any change goes through impact assessment: cost, timeline, risk, and business benefit. But do not treat original requirements as sacred if the business case changes. A requirement that made sense in March may be obsolete by September.

Common failure modes are predictable. The loudest department writes the requirements. IT writes technical requirements nobody uses. Finance assumes everything is a GL coding issue. Operations ignores reporting until go-live. Executives add AI or mobile without a use case. Vendors demo to the requirements instead of the process. The document gets signed but not understood. Avoid these by keeping requirements tied to decisions, roles, data, and test cases. A requirement without an owner is a future argument.

Work in short cycles per value stream. One cycle might cover order-to-cash, another procure-to-pay. Start with scope and interviews. Move to process mapping and data. Draft requirements and priorities. Review with owners. Then repeat for the next flow. The output should be a decision tool, not a book. A good requirements package has process maps, requirement statements, a data dictionary, an integration list, a nonfunctional checklist, priorities, a traceability matrix, and demo scripts.

The worst requirements gathering exercise I have seen produced a 300-page document with 1,200 line items and no priorities. The best produced a 20-page scenario guide, a data ownership map, and a list of ten decisions the ERP had to improve. The second team spent less time arguing about features and more time testing real transactions. If you cannot test it, it is not a requirement. If no one owns it, it will not survive contact with the project.
