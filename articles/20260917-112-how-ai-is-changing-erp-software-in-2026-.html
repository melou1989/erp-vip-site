How AI Is Changing ERP Software in 2026: Real vs Hype

PUBLISHED September 17, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Strategy

Two years ago the question from a buying committee was whether a vendor had AI. In 2026 the question is what it costs to run and who signs off when it writes to the general ledger. SAP ships Joule across S/4HANA Cloud, Oracle has pushed agents into Fusion Applications, Microsoft bundles Copilot through Dynamics 365, and NetSuite, Infor, Workday, Epicor, Sage and IFS all have something with a name attached. Feature parity on the marketing page is roughly complete. What differs now is where the AI touches a transaction an auditor will care about.

The most real deployment in the average enterprise is still document processing. Invoice capture has been OCR-driven for a decade; what changed is that large language models handle the messy tail — the non-PO invoice with a remittance stub, the vendor billing under three different entity names, the freight line nobody coded correctly the first time. Vendors now suggest GL coding, flag duplicate supplier records and route exceptions to a queue. Payback here is measurable and unglamorous, which is exactly why it survived the pilot phase. If you are looking for somewhere to start, start there.

Copilots embedded in the UI are the second wave. Ask which cost centers overran budget last quarter, in plain language, and get a chart back. The catch is that this only works if your chart of accounts, cost centers and dimensions are consistent across entities. Plenty of organizations discover during a copilot rollout that they have four definitions of region and no named owner for master data. Conversational BI is a data governance project wearing a chat interface.

Agentic AI is where the noise is loudest. Agents that chase overdue receivables, draft dispute responses, onboard suppliers, prepare accruals, reconcile intercompany balances. Some of this genuinely works in supervised mode: the agent assembles, a human approves. Very few companies run these unsupervised against the ledger, and the vendors' own reference calls tend to confirm that. The blocker is not model capability. An autonomous write to the general ledger is a control design decision, and your auditor will ask for the approval trail. 'The agent did it' satisfies nobody.

There is a reason SAP's clean core messaging and its AI messaging landed in the same breath. Models work well against standard schemas and standard processes, and degrade against the layers of user exits, Z-code and bolt-on modifications that accumulated over twenty years. That old customization is often undocumented, and the people who wrote it have retired. So the AI argument quietly becomes an argument for reimplementation rather than a technical lift-and-shift, which happens to line up with the ECC maintenance deadline. Whether that is a genuine technical constraint or a commercial one depends on your level of cynicism. Both are true.

Pricing is shifting under buyers' feet. Vendors are layering consumption meters — AI units, credits, per-agent metering — on top of per-user subscriptions. The result is a variable line item that scales with how much your staff talks to the system, not with how many people have accounts. Finance teams are not used to budgeting that way. Ask for consumption caps, usage telemetry and a straight answer about what happens when an agent loops and burns credits at three in the morning.

Where it breaks: a confidently wrong GL account on a material journal entry, a supplier matched to the wrong master record, a demand forecast that secretly reflects last year's seasonality because nobody retrained anything. Then there is shadow AI. AP clerks pasting remittance advice into public chatbots is a live data exposure path in more organizations than will admit it, and no vendor governance console covers what happens outside the ERP.

Regulation has started to bite. The EU AI Act's high-risk obligations phase in across 2026, and HR and workforce modules that screen or rank candidates sit squarely in that category — which matters if your ERP is also your HCM. SOX programs are being extended to cover automated journal entries and the models that produce them. The practical consequence is that model changes now need change management. If a vendor silently updates the model that codes your invoices, you need to know, and you need to retest.

Roles are moving too. ERP analysts spend less time in configuration screens and more time reviewing model output, defining exception rules and owning data quality. Systems integrators are repackaging accelerators for AI-assisted fit-gap, automated test script generation and migration mapping, which changes the shape of the billable hour without reliably shrinking the bill. Data migration still eats the schedule.

One question cuts through most of the vendor noise: name a customer who has run this feature in production through a full audit cycle. Not a design partner, not a pilot, not a co-innovation story. Someone whose external auditor has looked at the output and signed off. In 2026, the vendors who can answer that are the ones worth a second meeting.
