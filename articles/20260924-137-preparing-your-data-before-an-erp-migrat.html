Preparing Your Data Before an ERP Migration: A Practical Guide

PUBLISHED September 24, 2026 · GUIDE / Data Migration

An ERP migration is a data project that happens to include software. The demos show clean dashboards and approval flows. The actual work is deciding which customer record is correct, why the item master has three spellings of the same bracket, and whether that 2019 journal entry needs to exist in the new system. I have never seen a cutover fail because the new ERP could not post an invoice. I have seen it fail because the opening balances did not match.

Start with an inventory of every place data lives. That means the legacy ERP, yes, but also the Access database finance uses for rebates, the spreadsheets supply chain maintains for forecasts, the CRM with duplicate accounts, the WMS, the billing system, and whatever custom app someone built and left running. For each source, write down the system owner, record volume, refresh frequency, and whether the data is master data, transactional data, or reference data. If an extract has no owner, treat that as a risk, not a minor cleanup item.

Then decide what not to migrate. Historic transactions are the biggest trap. Most organizations do not need every sales order line from the past decade inside the new ERP. They need open items, balances, and enough history for audit, tax, and customer service. Put old detail in an archive or data warehouse with a read-only lookup path. Migrating everything creates noise, slows validation, and makes reconciliation harder. Check legal retention rules first, then draw a firm line.

Master data deserves its own workstream. Customer, vendor, item, employee, chart of accounts, cost center, and bank records feed almost every process. Deduplicate before mapping. Standardize names, addresses, tax IDs, payment terms, and currency. Item master is usually the worst: units of measure, conversions, categories, revision levels, BOMs, routings, and costing methods. If item data is wrong, inventory valuation and MRP results will be wrong in ways that take months to untangle. Assign a business data owner for each domain, not a single overworked analyst.

Mapping sounds mechanical until you open the spreadsheets. Legacy fields rarely map one-to-one. A free-text 'comments' field may need to split into structured attributes. Status codes have to be translated. Payment terms, tax codes, and GL accounts need value mappings. Write transformation rules down and keep a data dictionary. Include what happens when a field is blank, because blank is not a value. It is a decision someone has to make.

Profile the data before you load it anywhere. Run queries or use a data quality tool to find missing values, duplicates, invalid dates, orphaned records, and broken relationships. Sales orders without customers. Inventory transactions without items. GL entries that point to a cost center that no longer exists. Fix issues at the source when you can. When the legacy system is frozen or too fragile to touch, fix them in staging and document every change. Do not let the migration team become the silent data steward.

Open balances and cutover deserve a separate test cycle. You need an as-of date for AR, AP, inventory quantities and costs, GL balances, fixed assets, and open purchase orders. Reconcile those balances to the legacy trial balance and subledgers before cutover. Load open items, not the entire transaction history. Run mock loads into a non-production environment and compare record counts, control totals, and exception reports. Plan for rejects. There will be rejects.

Governance is what keeps the work from stalling. Data preparation starts early, before configuration decisions harden. It needs finance, supply chain, sales, and operations at the table, because they own the definitions. IT can extract and transform. IT cannot decide whether a customer is inactive or whether a part is obsolete. Freeze master data changes before cutover or define a controlled exception process. If the business cannot sign off on master data, the go-live date is fiction.

Vendor tools help with loading, not with cleaning. SAP has Migration Cockpit, Oracle has data management and loading tools, Microsoft Dynamics 365 has its data management framework, NetSuite uses CSV imports, Infor has ION, Epicor has DMT. Every one of them enforces a template and gives you error logs. None of them knows that 'Acme Corp' and 'Acme Corporation' are the same account. Tools make a bad load faster. They do not make bad data good.

If you only do a few things, do these: profile master data and open balances, name data owners in the business, map every field with a transformation rule, and run a full test load into a copy of the new system. The sooner you find the ugly records, the less expensive they are to fix. Cutover is not the time to discover that your item master has 40,000 inactive SKUs or that your customer list has 12,000 duplicates.
