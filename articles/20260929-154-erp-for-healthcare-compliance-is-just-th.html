ERP for Healthcare: Compliance Is Just the Starting Line

PUBLISHED September 29, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Compliance Strategy

Healthcare ERP is a messy category because most health systems do not buy one system for everything. A large hospital might run Epic for clinicals and revenue cycle, Workday or Oracle Fusion for finance and HR, Infor or SAP for supply chain, and a handful of niche tools for scheduling and grants. HIPAA compliance is the entry ticket: signed business associate agreements, role-based access, audit logs that cannot be edited, encryption at rest and in transit, and a documented breach response process. That is baseline. The harder question is whether the ERP actually connects labor, supplies, and service-line costs back to patient care.

Compliance requirements change by module. The HIPAA Security Rule applies to any system touching protected health information, including HR records that contain leave details or billing workflows that carry diagnosis codes. If you make medical devices or run clinical trials, FDA 21 CFR Part 11 brings electronic signature and audit trail rules. Pharmacies and distributors have DSCSA obligations for product tracing. Your ERP vendor must provide a BAA, but a BAA alone does not make the system compliant. I have seen teams assume a cloud provider's HIPAA-eligible services cover the whole stack. They do not. The configuration, user provisioning, and data retention policies are your responsibility.

Beyond checkboxes, the operational case for ERP in healthcare is cost and supply visibility. Finance wants to know the true cost of a joint replacement, including implant, OR time, nursing labor, and post-op care. Supply chain wants to track lot numbers and expiration dates so a recall does not turn into a manual spreadsheet hunt. HR wants to manage credentialing, shift differentials, and contract labor without three systems disagreeing. None of this works if the ERP cannot talk to the EHR. FHIR APIs help for clinical data, but supply chain, scheduling, and general ledger integrations still rely on custom interfaces, batch files, and middleware. Every interface is a future break point.

Vendor selection is where healthcare buyers get sold a story. Oracle, SAP, Infor, Workday, and Microsoft all have healthcare customers and referenceable deployments. Oracle Health can tie clinical and ERP data more tightly because it owns Cerner, but that also means a bet on Oracle's roadmap. Epic does not sell a traditional ERP, yet it dominates the clinical and revenue cycle layers, so any ERP you choose has to coexist with Epic, not replace it. Workday is strong in finance and HR but less proven in complex clinical supply chain. Infor has deep healthcare-specific supply chain and finance modules. SAP brings process depth and a heavy implementation footprint. Microsoft Dynamics 365 can fit mid-sized organizations but often needs add-ons for healthcare compliance and supply chain.

My advice for IT and finance leaders: run a real integration test before signing. Give each finalist two or three of your ugliest data flows—recalled lot to purchase order, contract labor to general ledger, patient charge to service-line cost—and make them show working interfaces in a sandbox. Ask where data resides, who can access it, how audit logs are retained, how custom fields survive upgrades, and what the exit plan looks like. Compliance is necessary, but the post-go-live pain is usually operational: broken interfaces, dirty master data, and users who keep their old spreadsheets. Pick the vendor whose healthcare references admit what went wrong, not the one with the cleanest demo.
