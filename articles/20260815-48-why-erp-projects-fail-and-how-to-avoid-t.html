Why ERP Projects Fail (And How to Avoid the Same Mistakes)

PUBLISHED August 15, 2026 · GUIDE / Implementation Strategy

Most ERP projects are not killed by the software. They get dragged down by something less dramatic: a data migration plan that nobody owns, a requirements list that keeps growing, and an executive team that stops attending steering meetings once the initial excitement wears off.

Data is where implementations go to die. I've seen teams spend weeks cleaning spreadsheets, only to load the data once and assume the job is done. Meanwhile, the business keeps moving — orders are entered, product codes are altered, customer records slip in with missing tax IDs. By go-live, the 'clean' data in the staging environment has nothing to do with reality. Fix this by assigning data ownership per domain, not to a central IT person, and by running go-live rehearsals using fresh transactions, not hand-picked sample rows.

'We can make the system do it that way' is the most expensive sentence in an ERP project. Every customization to preserve an old process adds testing burden, upgrade risk, and training time. The healthier question is whether the process itself should change. A fit-gap session isn't a negotiation; it exists to decide what the new standard should be, and where the exceptions are truly worth their future maintenance cost.

Change management fails when it's treated as a communication plan. It's not. People adapt to an ERP when they can see what their Tuesday morning actually looks like after go-live. That means role-based training, not videos, and it means having process owners who can say 'we won't build this' without being overridden by a VP who wants to keep a legacy report. Governance also needs a working escalation path. If you're making decisions in conference rooms, you're doing too much. Write them down, test them at the pilot site, and hold people to them.
