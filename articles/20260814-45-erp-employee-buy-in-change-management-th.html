ERP Employee Buy-In: Change Management That Actually Works

PUBLISHED August 14, 2026 · GUIDE / Change Management

The most common way ERP projects fail isn't a bad software choice. It's the quiet resistance of the people who have to use it daily. Finance teams might nod through steering committee meetings, then return to spreadsheets because they trust them more than the new system's reports. That's not a training problem. That's a change management problem.

When employees first hear about a new ERP from a vague company-wide email, they instantly imagine more work, less authority, and a system designed by consultants. The remedy is to involve a cross-section of users before the vendor is even selected — not just managers, but the accounts payable clerk who enters 300 invoices a month, the warehouse lead who lives in the receiving screen. Ask them what breaks, what they'd fix, and what they need from the new system. When people help shape the requirements, they start owning the outcome.

One practical pattern that works: pick skeptical, respected employees and make them the first people to see the system. Not the star performers who love anything shiny, but the veteran who complains the loudest. Give them early access, listen to their grievances, and let them be the ones to demonstrate the new processes to everyone else. They will be harder on the vendor than your PMO ever would be, and their eventual approval carries weight that no executive email can match.

Buy-in isn't a pre-go-live checkbox. It's a continuous loop. After the cutover, track which screens are used, which shortcuts get ignored, and which users still export to Excel. That last one is your early warning system. Build a feedback channel that isn't a complaint box. Show people that their specific requests — the field labels, the print layouts, the approval steps — actually made it into the system. The goal is to make the new ERP feel like their system, not the consultants' system. If you can do that, the resistance disappears on its own.
