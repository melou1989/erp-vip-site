ERP Requirements Gathering: A Step-by-Step Approach

PUBLISHED September 17, 2026 · GUIDE / Requirements

Most ERP projects do not fail because the software cannot do the work. They fail because the organization never agreed on what the work actually is. Requirements gathering is where that agreement gets built, or where it quietly falls apart. A good requirements phase produces a decision document, not a 400-page wish list. It tells you what must change, what can stay, what you will stop doing, and which vendor claims need proof.

Start with scope boundaries before interviews. Write down the legal entities, countries, currencies, business units, warehouses, plants, and sales channels in play. List the processes inside the first release and, just as important, the ones deliberately outside it. If someone says 'everything,' ask which process they are willing to keep in spreadsheets for another year. That sounds blunt, but it saves months. Scope is not a limitation; it is the only way to compare vendors fairly.

Pick a core team, not a committee. One process owner per domain—finance, supply chain, manufacturing, sales, procurement, HR, IT, data, reporting. Each owner can make calls for their area and has a deputy. Add a project manager who controls the calendar and a business analyst who writes things down in a way normal humans can read. Executives sponsor. They do not run workshops. When a disagreement reaches them, they decide within days, not weeks.

Interview by process, not by department. A department interview produces turf protection. A process interview follows a transaction from trigger to posting: quote to cash, procure to pay, record to report, plan to produce, hire to retire, case to resolution. Ask what starts the process, what data enters, who approves, what exceptions occur, what reports come out, and what happens when it breaks. Then ask for the ugly examples: credits, returns, drop-shipments, intercompany loans, consignment, rework, recalls, union rules, rebates. The exceptions are where ERP fit gets decided.

Map current state at the right altitude. You do not need a 900-box flowchart for every task. You need enough detail to see handoffs, delays, duplicate entry, approval bottlenecks, and controls. Use a swimlane for each core process. Mark steps that exist only because the current system cannot do something. Mark steps that exist because a customer, auditor, or regulator requires them. The first group is often a candidate for elimination. The second group is a requirement.

Separate requirements into categories that force honest trade-offs. Must-have: without it, you cannot invoice, ship, pay, comply, or close the books. Should-have: significant pain, but a workaround exists for a release. Nice-to-have: useful, not worth delaying go-live. Also capture nonfunctional requirements: volume, response time expectations, availability, audit trails, segregation of duties, data retention, localization, mobile access, integration frequency. These rarely show up in demos, then sink cutover.

Write requirements as outcomes, not software features. 'The system must support returns' is useless. 'A customer service rep can authorize a return against the original shipment, choose replacement or credit, and see inventory availability before confirming' is testable. Include the actor, trigger, data, business rule, and expected result. Avoid naming a vendor's module or transaction code unless you are already in contract. Naming features too early narrows thinking and invites demos that look good but do not fit.

Handle data and integrations before you fall in love with a vendor. List every source system, every interface, direction, frequency, data owner, and error-handling rule. Be honest about master data quality. If customer names, item numbers, supplier records, or charts of accounts are inconsistent, ERP will not clean them up by magic. Data migration is not an IT side task. It is a business process with owners, cutover rules, and reconciliation. Vendors will ask who is responsible. Have an answer.

Use a fit-gap matrix, but keep it argumentative. For each requirement, record priority, process area, current pain, desired outcome, and vendor response. During scripted demos, give vendors your scenarios in advance. Ask them to show the exact steps, the approval path, the report output, and what happens when the data is wrong. Watch for 'we can customize that' without a cost or upgrade impact. A gap is not automatically a reason to reject a vendor. It is a reason to price the workaround, estimate the risk, and decide whether the process should change instead.

Validate with a conference room pilot, not a beauty contest. Take one real end-to-end scenario from your business, load a representative slice of data, and run it in the proposed system. Include a messy exception, an approval, a reporting requirement, and an integration handoff. If the vendor cannot do this without heroics, believe what you see. This step also exposes whether your own requirements are contradictory. That is cheaper to find before contract than during hypercare.

Get sign-off in layers. The process owners sign their area. The project sponsor signs the scope, priorities, and assumptions. IT signs the integration, data, security, and support model. Legal and compliance sign retention, segregation of duties, and regulatory reporting. Then put the signed requirements under change control. New requirements can enter, but they need an owner, a priority, and a trade-off. Otherwise the baseline becomes fiction by the first steering committee meeting.

Maintain a living requirements register. During configuration, testing, and cutover, you will discover things you missed. Track them in one place with status, decision, and owner. Don't let decisions live in email threads or a consultant's notebook. When someone asks why a process works a certain way two years after go-live, the register is the answer. If you cannot trace a configuration back to a requirement, either the requirement is missing or the configuration is unnecessary.
