ERP for Nonprofits: Fund Accounting Explained

PUBLISHED October 01, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Fund Accounting

Fund accounting is not a reporting style you bolt onto a standard chart of accounts. It is a control model. In a nonprofit, money arrives with strings: a grant restricted to a specific program, a gift for scholarships, an endowment whose principal cannot be spent, a federal award with a period of performance. Each fund acts like a self-balancing set of books. The balance sheet does not show shareholder equity; it shows net assets without donor restrictions and net assets with donor restrictions. If the ERP cannot enforce that separation at transaction entry, someone will spend restricted money on rent and the finance team will spend month-end moving expenses around in a spreadsheet.

The daily mechanics matter. When a restricted grant check lands, the system should debit cash and credit revenue in net assets with donor restrictions. As program staff incur allowable costs, the ERP releases the restriction and moves the revenue to net assets without donor restrictions. A generic ERP with a fund dimension can tag the revenue, but tagging is not releasing. Without an automated release mechanism, restricted net assets stay overstated and program budgets look underfunded. Interfund transfers need due-to and due-from accounts, not a single journal entry that hides which fund owes which.

Functional expense reporting is another place where general ledgers strain. Audited financial statements and Form 990 require expenses split among program services, management and general, and fundraising. Most nonprofits do not have a payroll clerk who can code every hour to a function. They allocate salaries, occupancy, IT, depreciation, and shared administrative costs across functions using drivers such as headcount, square footage, or time studies. A fund accounting system should perform those allocations when payroll posts, keep the original cost visible, and let auditors trace the allocated amount back to the source transaction. If allocations live only in an Excel workbook, the audit trail is whatever the preparer remembers.

Grants add a second layer. A grant budget is not the annual operating budget. It has a start and end date, a restricted purpose, a required match or cost share, an indirect cost rate, and often a billing or drawdown schedule. Federal awards bring Uniform Guidance requirements: time and effort documentation, procurement standards, subrecipient monitoring, and a Single Audit above the threshold. A mid-market ERP with project accounting can track expenses against a grant, but grant billing, revenue recognition, and compliance reporting may need a grant management module or a purpose-built integration. Trying to run federal grants through a project code alone usually ends with a reconciliation spreadsheet.

Reporting expectations are different, too. Nonprofit boards and finance committees ask for a statement of financial position by fund, a statement of activities with restriction releases, a budget-to-actual by grant, and a functional expense report. Auditors ask for a net asset rollforward by restriction and a schedule of expenditures of federal awards. Donors ask whether their gift was spent as intended. These are not ad hoc reports; they are the accounting structure. When the ERP stores funds as departments, restrictions as classes, grants as projects, and donors as customers, the same transaction can be reported four ways. That flexibility is useful until the combinations multiply and report writers quit.

The vendor landscape reflects that split. Blackbaud Financial Edge NXT, MIP Fund Accounting, Aplos, Fund EZ, and Serenic Navigator grew up around nonprofit fund accounting, so funds, restrictions, and functional expenses are native concepts. Sage Intacct, NetSuite, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central, and Acumatica can support nonprofits through dimensions, classes, projects, and add-on modules. The nonprofit-specific suites tend to understand donor restrictions and grant reporting out of the box. The broader ERPs tend to handle multi-entity consolidation, inventory, global currencies, complex revenue, and manufacturing better. Neither path is automatically cheaper. Pricing varies significantly by vendor, user count, module mix, and deployment size.

Integration is where fund accounting and fundraising collide. The CRM holds pledges, recurring gifts, event registrations, memberships, and donor designations. The ERP holds revenue, receivables, restricted net assets, and grant expenses. If the two systems do not reconcile nightly, the development office will report one number and finance will report another. Look for a documented mapping between donor restrictions and GL funds or classes, a clear process for pledge payments and write-offs, and an audit trail that connects a deposit to a donor and a restriction. For larger nonprofits, Salesforce Nonprofit Cloud or Blackbaud Raiser's Edge may sit alongside a separate fund accounting ERP. In smaller shops, an all-in-one system may be the only realistic option.

Evaluate with a script, not a checklist. Ask each vendor to show a restricted grant from award letter to budget, expense entry, restriction release, grant billing, indirect cost allocation, functional expense report, and fund-level statement. Ask how the system handles a multi-year pledge, an endowment with board designations, a fiscal year that differs from the grant period, and a federal award with cost share. Then ask how many custom reports the implementation team has built for similar nonprofits. The answer tells you whether the ERP understands fund accounting or whether your finance team will become the fund accounting module.
