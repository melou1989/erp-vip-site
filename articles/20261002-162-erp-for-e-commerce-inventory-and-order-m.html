ERP for E-commerce: Inventory and Order Management

PUBLISHED October 02, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Inventory & Orders

E-commerce inventory and order management looks like a simple extension of ERP until you actually run it. You sell on Shopify, Amazon, Walmart, and maybe a physical store. An order comes in with three items. One is in the warehouse, one is drop-shipped from a supplier, and one is out of stock. The customer expects a single shipment or at least clear communication. Your ERP sees three separate sales orders and a backorder. That gap is where most e-commerce ERP projects fail.

Most mid-market ERPs have some e-commerce story. NetSuite, Acumatica, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central, SAP Business One, Odoo. The connectors vary. Some are native, some are built by partners, some are just CSV imports. The critical question is sync frequency. If inventory updates every hour, you will oversell on a flash sale. Real-time or near-real-time reservation matters more than any feature checklist.

Order orchestration is the harder problem. E-commerce orders are not clean. They have multiple ship-to addresses, gift messages, promotional codes, and split payments. Returns are worse. A customer buys three sizes, keeps one, returns two. Your ERP needs to restock the returned items, issue a refund to the original payment method, and adjust inventory across channels. Many ERPs treat this as a credit memo. That works for accounting, not for operations.

Then there is the architecture question. Do you plug your e-commerce platform directly into the ERP? Or do you insert a dedicated order management system like Brightpearl, Cin7, or Linnworks? In practice, high-volume e-commerce often breaks a pure ERP. The ERP is good at financials, procurement, and manufacturing. It is not good at real-time order routing, multi-location fulfillment, or marketplace-specific rules. A middleware layer adds latency and another point of failure, but it can also save you from customizing the ERP into a mess.

If you insist on one system, look for ERPs that treat commerce as a first-class citizen. NetSuite and Acumatica have strong native capabilities, but they come with complexity and cost. Business Central is improving, especially with Shopify, but high-volume or multi-channel operations may still need add-ons. Odoo is flexible but you will be writing code for edge cases. The CFO will care about margin by channel, COGS accuracy, and revenue recognition. The IT director will care about API rate limits, webhooks, and idempotency. Operations will care about pick, pack, ship, and exception handling. Those three sets of requirements rarely align perfectly.

So test with real data before you commit. Simulate a peak day: 10x normal order volume, a marketplace outage, a return spike. See how the ERP handles inventory reservations, order splitting, and refunds. Ask vendors to show you a return that spans two channels. If they demo only a clean web order, you are not seeing the hard part. E-commerce does not slow down for your ERP implementation. It accelerates.
