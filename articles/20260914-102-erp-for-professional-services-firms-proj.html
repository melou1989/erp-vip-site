ERP for Professional Services Firms: Project Accounting Needs

PUBLISHED September 14, 2026 · INSIGHTS / Buyer's Guide

Most ERPs can put a project number on a purchase order. That is not project accounting. For a consultancy, architecture firm, IT services shop, or engineering group, the project is the operating unit. Revenue arrives through time and materials, fixed fees, milestones, retainers, or progress billings. Costs arrive through labor, expenses, subcontractors, and overhead allocations. The ERP has to track unbilled WIP, deferred revenue, contract assets, and contract liabilities without a spreadsheet reconciliation every month. If the system only recognizes revenue when an invoice goes out, you are flying blind on margin.

Time and expense entry is the daily beating heart of the system. If consultants hate the timesheet, data quality collapses, and every downstream metric—utilization, realized rate, project profitability—gets soft. Look for mobile entry, draft timesheets, approval routing, billable and nonbillable categories, multiple rate cards, split billing, expense markups, and payroll integration. The ERP should handle rate changes mid-project, write-ups and write-downs, and billable expense rules without custom code. Firms that outgrow QuickBooks or a standalone PSA often do so because billing rules and revenue schedules have become a patchwork of exports.

Revenue recognition is where professional services ERPs separate from generic financial systems. Under ASC 606 and IFRS 15, many contracts require over-time recognition, cost-to-cost measures, milestone progress, or labor-hour inputs. Change orders and contract modifications need to flow into the revenue schedule, not sit in a project manager's email. Auditors want a clear trail from contract to performance obligation to recognized revenue to invoice. Some ERP suites automate this well; others leave it to Excel and a brave accountant. That gap matters more as firms take on larger, multi-element engagements.

Resource planning is adjacent to project accounting, and the two need to share data. An ERP can tell you what a project cost last month. It cannot always tell you who is available next week, what skills the next phase needs, or whether the pipeline will overload a practice area. Workday, Oracle, SAP, and Microsoft Dynamics 365 Project Operations approach this from an enterprise suite angle. Certinia, Kantata, and Kimble are PSA-first tools with accounting hooks. Deltek Vantagepoint and BST Global are built for architecture and engineering workflows. Deltek Costpoint and Unanet are common in government contracting, where indirect rates, DCAA compliance, and provisional billing are not optional. Sage Intacct, NetSuite, and Acumatica can work for mid-market firms, but depth varies sharply by billing model and revenue recognition needs.

Billing complexity is usually the first thing to break. A firm may bill one client monthly by retainer, another by milestone, another by time and materials with a not-to-exceed cap, and a fourth by percentage of completion. The invoice may need to split across cost centers, currencies, entities, or intercompany arrangements. The ERP should generate those invoices from the same project data that drives revenue recognition and cost reporting. When billing lives in a different tool, finance spends the first week of every month reconciling unbilled receivables and explaining why project margin does not match the GL.

Project profitability reporting has to go deeper than a P&L by customer. You need labor cost with burden, direct expenses, subcontractor costs, indirect allocations, and write-offs. Managers want budget versus actual, estimate to complete, estimate at completion, and change order impact. Executives want realized rates by consultant, service line, office, and client. Partners want to see which engagements are quietly subsidized by unbilled scope. A good ERP makes those views self-service. A bad one produces a month-end report that nobody trusts.

Selection advice: do not buy a generic ERP and bolt on project accounting as an afterthought. Also do not buy a standalone PSA and assume finance can live in a separate accounting system forever. Map your worst contract to a demo script. Include a fixed-fee project with change orders, a T&M project with multiple rate cards, a retainer with rollover, a multi-currency engagement, and a government contract with indirect rates. Watch how the system handles WIP, revenue, billing, and reporting. If the vendor cannot show that without a custom demo, believe what you see. Pricing varies significantly by vendor, user count, deployment model, and the number of legal entities, but the cost of a poor fit shows up in monthly reconciliation labor and margin leakage.
